Abstract
Container terminals use simulation to evaluate operational changes without disrupting live
operations, but these models are commonly constructed and calibrated by hand. Data-aware
process simulation instead derives executable models from event logs; evidence from physical
logistics remains limited.
This thesis evaluates the approach for truck operations at HHLA Container T erminal Bur-
chardkai. T wo months of data were transformed into an event log of 89,460 visits and divided
temporally into discovery and held-out periods. The white-box ProSiT framework was evalu-
ated through three configurations: distribution-only, workload-blind process-state rules, and
rules with workload features. An apparent parallelism defect was traced to timestamp-based
event reordering. Preserving explicit XES order produced a compact sequential Petri net used
as the common executable control flow.
The net fits 99.994 % of 17,892 held-out visits. A separate repair removes a silent gate-only
bypass, raising precision from 0.7558 to 0.7939 while lowering generalisation from 0.9413 to
0.8859. The final scenario baseline reproduces arrival spacing closely and parts of yard service
timing, but mean turnaround is 8.17 minutes too short and P90 is 17.31 minutes too short. The
8.17-minute test-period gap decomposes into a 2.45-minute increase in the observed mean be-
tween periods and a 5.72-minute simulation deficit relative to the training-period mean. Paired
ten-seed ablation shows that workload-blind rules improve activity incidence and suppress
a rarely sampled invalid bypass, while their small turnaround-distance improvement is unre-
solved. Adding workload state yields no resolved service-time gain and worsens both activity
incidence and turnaround distance. The automatically discovered RMG concurrency values
required a documented domain correction to the physical maximum of three cranes per block.
T wo reproducible what-if experiments produce no resolved mean-turnaround response. The
20 % demand increase leaves the most-loaded held-out RMG block at 0.506 nominal mean util-
isation, while the model also lacks the spatial mechanisms needed for physical counterfactual
claims.
The thesis contributes a layered validity audit separating event semantics, control-flow lan-
guage, historical fidelity, temporal transfer, physical resource–space semantics, and scenario re-
sponse. Data-aware simulation is valuable here as an auditable historical and diagnostic model,
but direct operational decision support requires validated location, movement, resource, and
queueing mechanisms. With these extensions, the approach could compare truck-slot rates and
handling priorities under forecast terminal conditions before their implementation.

Introduction
Container terminals connect maritime and hinterland transport networks. Higher container
volumes and larger vessels require terminal operators to make many tactical and operational
decisions under uncertainty. These decisions concern truck dispatching, yard-block allocation,
resource use, congestion, equipment failures, and temporary infrastructure outages. Experi-
ments in an operating terminal are often costly, disruptive, or not feasible. Simulation offers a
way to assess decisions without interrupting terminal operations.
1.1 Motivation
Simulation is used for decision support in container-terminal planning and operations research.
Its operational purpose is prospective: a terminal can compare how alternative process execu-
tions and operating conditions affect specific handling patterns before changing live operations.
For example, when high yard utilisation is forecast, a terminal may compare lower truck-slot
release rates, alternative resource allocations, or policies that favour receive or delivery opera-
tions, and evaluate their effects on activity-specific service, pre-service delay, and total truck
turnaround. Simulation can likewise assess infrastructure investments, scheduling approaches,
truck appointment systems, and terminal redesigns before implementation. Traditional ap-
proaches mostly use manually developed Discrete Event Simulation (DES) models, agent-based
models, or digital twins. Building and maintaining these models requires domain knowledge,
1

===== PDF PAGE 11 =====
manual work, and calibration [3, 4, 5, 6].
Process Mining research has studied how simulation models can be built from event logs.
Earlier work showed that control-flow behaviour, activity durations, arrival patterns, resource
allocations, and routing probabilities can be discovered from process data and used in exe-
cutable simulation models [7]. Data-aware process simulationextends this idea. It uses context
attributes, case information, process states, resource characteristics, and decision logic [8, 9, 10,
11].
Machine-learning and deep-learning methods can learn complicated behavior, but their re-
sults are often difficult to inspect, modify, and validate. Hybrid and white-box approaches
combine interpretable models with data-driven behaviour discovery [12, 13]. ProSiT uses prob-
abilistic white-box predictors to build simulation models that can be inspected and configured
for what-if analysis [13].
Evidence on automatically discovered simulation models in logistics systems is still limited.
Within the literature reviewed for this thesis, this intersection remains under-evaluated, with
most studies still centred on business processes in healthcare, financial services, and administra-
tion [14, 10]. Container terminal truck operations involve physical resource constraints, conges-
tion, infrastructure dependencies, spatial interactions, and changing process contexts. It is not
yet clear how well a discovered data-aware simulation model can reproduce these conditions
and support decisions. This thesis studies its fidelity, structural applicability, and limitations
using event logs from real container terminal truck operations.
This problem is also visible in the wider research landscape. A recent review of 105 studies
at the intersection of Process Mining and simulation finds that validation remains heteroge-
neous, that queue and resource modelling often requires information outside conventional
event logs, and that fully automated model generation remains uncommon [15]. W ork in pro-
duction and logistics similarly stresses that processing timestamps alone do not reliably expose
transport, waiting, inventory, and physical resource interactions; simulation-ready reconstruc-
tion may require harmonised data from several operational levels and an object-oriented view
[16, 17]. The CTB case therefore provides a demanding empirical test of both the promise and
the support boundary of automatic simulation discovery. Adjacent ASMG research has already
shown that physical-system structure and parameters can be reconstructed from manufactur-
ing data, while emphasising purpose-dependent abstraction and multi-object material flows
[18, 19, 20, 21]. Within the literature reviewed for this thesis, the unresolved intersection re-
mains a temporally validated, data-aware executable model discovered from real terminal event
data and subjected to explicit physical and what-if validity checks.

1.2 Problem Statement
Container terminal operators make decisions about capacity, truck handling, yard use, policies,
and infrastructure changes. Experiments in a real terminal are often not feasible or involve op-
erational risk, so these decisions are often studied with simulation. Most container-terminal
simulation models are built manually. Their construction and maintenance require domain
knowledge and modelling work. They may include subjective assumptions about process be-
haviour, resource interactions, and routing logic [4, 5].
Data-aware process simulation discovers simulation models from historical event logs. This
may reduce modelling work and keep the model closer to the recorded process [22, 11, 10]. Be-
fore such a model can support decisions, it must reproduce observed operations, remain con-
figurable, produce stable results, and represent the mechanisms through which a proposed in-
tervention can affect the relevant KPI. A model intended to recommend a truck-slot rate under
forecast congestion must, for example, propagate changed arrivals through resource contention
and must keep receive and delivery effects distinguishable. Earlier studies have shown that data-
aware simulation can be applied to business processes [10, 14]. Its use in logistics systems with
congestion, competing resources, and context-dependent behaviour is less clear. This thesis
evaluates the model’s fidelity, use, and limitations for container-terminal truck operations.
1.3 Research Objective and Contributions
The objective of this thesis is to evaluate the fidelity, structural applicability, and limitations of
data-aware process simulation for container terminal truck operations. The study investigates
how far a simulation model derived from historical event data using ProSiT can reproduce un-
seen truck-handling behaviour at the Container T erminal Burchardkai (CTB), and where the
transfer of process-simulation discovery to a physical logistics environment reaches its limits.
Trustworthiness motivates this evaluation, but it is not treated as a property that can be estab-
lished from a single historical case study.
More specifically, the thesis evaluates whether the control-flow representation derived from
the training data remains applicable to a later held-out period while respecting the physical se-
quence of a truck visit. It then assesses the historical fidelity of the resulting simulator, examines
whether increasing the contextual expressiveness of the ProSiT configuration improves this fi-
delity, and investigates how the data-aware model responds to transparent operational inter-
ventions. T ogether, these analyses are intended to distinguish between reproducing historical

===== PDF PAGE 13 =====
behaviour, constructing an executable simulation model, and supporting credible what-if rea-
soning.
The thesis contributes an empirical evaluation of data-aware process simulation in container
terminal truck operations. First, it examines the transfer of a recent process-simulation discov-
ery approach to a logistics setting characterised by physical sequencing, shared resources, tem-
poral variation, and congestion. Second, it identifies methodological challenges that arise when
automatically inferred process and resource abstractions do not fully correspond to known
physical constraints, requiring explicit validation and, where justified, documented domain
adaptations. Third, it compares different levels of contextual model expressiveness on unseen
temporal data rather than assuming that additional data awareness necessarily improves simula-
tion quality. Finally, two operational interventions are used to investigate both the capabilities
and the boundaries of the resulting white-box model for what-if analysis and decision support.
The intended decision-support logic is made explicit: forecast terminal conditions define a sce-
nario, candidate slot or resource policies are simulated, and their consequences are compared at
both activity and case level. The case study evaluates how far the discovered model can support
that logic without treating mere scenario executability as evidence of physical counterfactual
validity.
The central methodological contribution is a layered validity audit. Rather than reducing
simulation quality to one distance between a simulated log and a future log, the audit separates
event-order semantics, control-flow language, state-conditioned stochastic behaviour, tempo-
ral transfer, physical resource–space semantics, and scenario response. A model can pass an
earlier layer and fail a later one: near-perfect replay fitness, for example, does not show that
travel, queues, or an intervention mechanism are represented. This separation turns residual
errors and null scenario responses into diagnostic results and defines the strongest claim that
the current model can support. This operationalises, for one physical-logistics application, the
recent call for validation procedures that jointly consider process-model quality, historical out-
comes, and stakeholder-relevant semantics rather than relying on an isolated fit measure [15].
The empirical study is based on operational CTB truck-event data and combines process
analysis, simulation-model generation, temporal hold-out validation, multi-seed simulation,
and controlled scenario experiments. The corresponding research questions specify how these
objectives are evaluated in the remainder of the thesis.

1.4 Research Questions
T o achieve the research objective, the thesis addresses the following research questions:
1. RQ1: T o what extent can a ProSiT simulation model discovered from historical CTB
truck-operation data reproduce the observed behaviour of an unseen temporal hold-
out?
2. RQ2: T o what extent can the discovered process structure represent held-out CTB truck-
handling behaviour while respecting the physical sequential constraints of a truck visit?
3. RQ3: How does increasing the contextual expressiveness of the simulation model affect
held-out simulation fidelity in the CTB truck process?
4. RQ4: How does the discovered simulation model respond to transparent operational
interventions, and what do these responses reveal about its suitability and limitations for
what-if decision support?

Background and Related W ork
2.1 Container Terminal Operations
Seaport container terminals connect maritime and hinterland transport by linking deep-sea and
feeder vessels with road, rail, and inland-waterway services. Large vessels can carry more than
20,000 containers, requiring high handling volumes to be coordinated while vessel and landside
turnaround times remain short [4]. Since the 1960s, the standardised container has become the
principal unit of global freight transport. Throughput is reported in twenty-foot equivalent
units (TEU): a 20-foot container equals one TEU and a 40-foot container equals two [23]. The
resulting coordination choices affect berth time, truck turnaround, terminal throughput, and
service quality [4, 5]. This thesis studies HHLA Container T erminal Burchardkai (CTB), the
largest of HHLA’s Hamburg terminals [1].
2.1.1 Container-Terminal Systems and Truck Operations
Container terminals are complex logistics systems because many resources, transport modes,
and operational processes interact during daily operations [4, 5].
Container Flows Within a seaport terminal, containers move between transport modes
and storage locations. A container may arrive by vessel, truck, or rail and later leave by one of
these modes. The terminal coordinates flows between the seaside and landside [23]. Hartmann
distinguishes deep-sea vessels, feeder vessels, trucks, and rail as the four main transport modes.
7

===== PDF PAGE 17 =====
Their combinations create many possible paths through the terminal [23]. Most containers are
stored in the yard before they leave the terminal. T erminals must manage both transport pro-
cesses and storage allocation [4]. Figure2.1shows the basic connection between the waterside,
the yard, and hinterland transport.
Figure 2.1: Container flows between the waterside, yard, and hinterland. Source: Hamburger Hafen und Logistik AG [1].
Yard Components and Resources Container terminals consist of several connected op-
erational areas. Bett et al. distinguish the quayside, yard, and landside. The quayside includes
berths and quay cranes for loading and unloading vessels. The yard stores containers between
transport steps, while the landside connects the terminal to trucks and rail operations [6]. This
thesis focuses on three CTB yard areas. Most containers are stored in 22 automatic blocks op-
erated by Rail Mounted Gantry Cranes (RMGs), with up to three RMGs installed per block
between the waterside and landside. A centrally located manual yard uses mobile handling
equipment and permits storage decisions to respond to berth assignment, workload, and equip-
ment availability. Empty containers are kept in a separate yard, where they can be managed as
interchangeable inventory within a shipping line’s fleet and stacked densely by reach stackers.
Separating this inventory prevents empty units without a fixed delivery order from obstructing
full containers linked to active transport orders.
Trucks can pick up or deliver containers in all three yard areas. In each area, they wait at
a transfer point for the assigned handling resource. A resource shortage in one area can delay
other terminal operations [4].

deliveries and collections during the same visit.

2.1.2 Simulation-Based Decision Support in Container Terminals
T erminal managers make decisions about truck appointments, resources, equipment schedules,
maintenance, and yard operations while the terminal remains active. Because terminal pro-
cesses are connected, local decisions can change waiting times, resource use, throughput, and
service quality. Even experienced terminal managers face uncertainty. Changes to procedures
or capacity can create congestion, increase handling times, or reduce service quality. Simula-
tion allows managers and researchers to evaluate such changes without affecting live operations.
Common applications include Truck Appointment Systems (TAS), equipment allocation, re-
source planning, yard management, and storage strategies. Simulation is useful for congestion,
bottlenecks, and trade-offs that are difficult to analyse directly. Studies have shown its value for
truck turnaround time, resource use, and terminal performance [25, 6, 26]. Practical use is still
limited by model complexity, costs, and missing technical expertise in terminal teams.
Different simulation approaches are used in terminal research. Discrete Event Simulation
(DES) is the most common because terminal operations can be modelled as events such as truck
arrivals, gate processing, container handling, and equipment use. System Dynamics, Agent-
Based Modelling, and traffic simulation are used for other operational and strategic questions
[27, 28, 26]. Traditional simulation projects have limits. Building a model requires domain

knowledge, process understanding, and much manual work. Data collection, implementation,
calibration, and validation take time. Manual models depend on expert assumptions, which
may describe the intended process rather than its actual execution [7].
These differences can reduce the quality of simulation results. Manual models must be
maintained and calibrated when processes, workloads, and operating rules change. This work
is costly and requires technical support. At the same time, terminal information systems record
large amounts of operational data. Process Mining can use these data to discover, analyse, and
monitor processes from their recorded execution. This may reduce manual modelling work
and make simulation models closer to the observed process.
2.2 Operational Data and Event Logs
2.2.1 Information Systems and Event Semantics
Operational information systems record transactions in support of daily work, although their
databases are rarely organised as complete process histories. Enterprise Resource Planning,
Customer Relationship Management, and Manufacturing Execution Systems fulfil this role in
many organisations. In a container terminal, the corresponding evidence is distributed across
the T erminal Operating System (TOS), Gate Operating System (GOS), Y ard Management Sys-
tem (YMS), and equipment-control systems. A truck arrival, a storage assignment, or the com-
pletion of a container move may therefore be recorded in different systems and at different
levels of detail. At CTB, historically grown naming conventions add a further integration prob-
lem; Chapter3describes how these records are reconciled.
Process Mining turns such transactions into a consistent sequence ofevents. An event is one
recorded occurrence, whereas itsactivity identifies the type of operation, such asGate In , a
container delivery, orGate Out . Repeated occurrences of the same activity are therefore sep-
arate events. Events are associated with acase, the object whose process execution is followed.
Depending on the analysis, that object might be an order, container, or vehicle visit; through-
out this thesis, one truck visit defines one case. This choice fixes the process boundary from
the truck’s admission to its exit and allows all recorded operations of that visit to be analysed
together.
Each event must state at least which case it belongs to, which activity occurred, and when
it occurred. Performance-oriented Process Mining and simulation additionally distinguish an
observed start timestamp from an observed completion timestamp. Their difference is the ac-

tivity’sservice time. A third temporal concept,enablement, denotes the moment at which the
process-model state allows the activity to begin. CTB does not record enablement directly; it is

ablement and observed activity startwaiting time [13]. In this physical setting it is interpreted

Further attributes describe the circumstances of an execution. Resource or equipment iden-

only when their values would already be available at the corresponding simulated decision
point. This information-timing requirement prevents later outcomes from being used as if
they were known in advance.

2.3 Process Mining Foundations
2.3.1 Process Mining and Its Analytical Perspectives
The operational records described in Section2.2 can be analysed at different levels. Business
Intelligence summarises past and current performance through reports, dashboards, and key
performance indicators. Data Mining identifies patterns, classifications, and predictive relation-
ships, but conventional methods often treat records as independent observations. Process Min-
ing instead retains their ordering and connects the event data with an explicit process model. It
can therefore reconstruct how work was executed, monitor process performance, and identify
bottlenecks or deviations [7, 29].
Process Mining combines four complementary perspectives [7]. The control-flow perspec-
tive reconstructs which activities occur, their ordering relations, and the alternative paths fol-
lowed by different cases. The time perspective adds activity durations, waiting and throughput
times, and recurring bottlenecks. T ogether they describe how a process progresses and how its
performance develops over time.
The resource perspective relates execution to people, equipment, or other operational units
and examines how work is allocated among them. The data perspective connects behaviour
with case and event attributes, such as object type, yard location, or operational context, that
may influence routing or duration. These perspectives are analytically distinct but describe
the same recorded process; their joint use links process structure with timing, resources, and
operating circumstances.
2.3.2 Process Discovery and Model Representations
The objective of process discovery is to construct a process model from an event log without
a predefined process model [29]. The output should reproduce the observed behaviour. De-
pending on the discovery algorithm, it can be a Petri net, process tree, BPMN model, or directly-
follows graph [7]. The Alpha Miner is an early algorithm for deriving causal dependencies and
13

===== PDF PAGE 23 =====
parallel behaviour. The Heuristics Miner adds observed frequencies and is consequently more
tolerant of noise and infrequent behaviour. The Inductive Miner used in this thesis recursively
decomposes the log through sequence, exclusive-choice, parallel, and loop cuts, producing a
sound block-structured model while allowing infrequent behaviour to be filtered [30].
Process discovery shows how a recorded process was executed, rather than how it was ex-
pected to operate [29].
A directly-follows graph (DFG) summarises which activity directly follows another and can
annotate nodes and arcs with observed frequencies or performance values. This makes the DFG
useful for inspecting dominant flows, rare connections, and repetitions. It does not, however,
define complete execution semantics: a path that can be drawn through a DFG is not necessarily
a valid process execution [29].
Process trees and Petri nets provide such semantics. A process tree represents behaviour
through nested sequence, exclusive-choice, parallel, and loop operators. The Inductive Miner
discovers this block-structured representation and can convert it into a sound Petri net [30].
The Petri net distinguishes places, visible activity transitions, and silent routing transitions; its
markings determine which activity may execute next. Consequently, the DFG and Petri net
answer complementary questions: the DFG shows what was frequent in the log, whereas the
Petri net states what the model allows. The CTB instances of both views are therefore presented
separately and interpreted in Section4.4.
2.3.3 Conformance Checking and Process Enhancement
Conformance checking compares recorded process executions with an existing model and iden-
tifies deviations that may indicate exceptional cases, inefficiencies, compliance problems, or
outdated documentation [31]. Discovered models are commonly assessed through four related
quality criteria [31, 29]. Fitnessmeasures how much observed behaviour the model can replay,
whereasprecisionpenalises additional behaviour allowed without support in the log.General-
isationrewards a model that captures plausible underlying behaviour instead of reproducing
only the observed sample, andsimplicityfavours structural economy when competing models
explain the data sufficiently well. The criteria must be interpreted jointly: a perfectly fitting
model may overgeneralise, while a highly precise model may merely memorise the available
cases.

2.4 Business Process Simulation
2.4.1 Fundamentals of Business Process Simulation
Business Process Simulation (BPS) uses executable models to study process behaviour in a vir-
tual environment. Organisations can run these models under controlled conditions without
disturbing live operations [33]. Simulation can estimate throughput times, waiting times, re-
source use, service levels, and costs before a change is implemented. It is often used forwhat-if
analysis. Examples include changing the number of resources, routing rules, workload, or op-
erational policies. The results of different scenarios can then be compared [33]. BPS is used in
manufacturing, healthcare, logistics, supply chains, and business-process management. Process
Mining allows such models to be discovered and calibrated from recorded process executions

===== PDF PAGE 25 =====
[7, 22].
2.4.2 Business Process Simulation Models
A simulation model is a simplified representation of a process built from five connected ele-
ments [33, 7]. Its control flow defines the available activities, their possible order, and alter-
native execution paths, using a formalism such as a Petri net, BPMN model, process tree, or
workflow graph [29, 33]. The arrival process determines when new cases enter and is commonly
represented by a distribution of inter-arrival times; changing this input alters workload, wait-
ing, and resource utilisation.
Resources provide the capacity that executes activities and may represent employees, vehi-
cles, cranes, or other equipment. Their availability, capabilities, calendars, and allocation rules
determine how cases compete for service [33]. Activity-duration models then represent pro-
cessing and waiting times, usually through probability distributions fitted to historical obser-
vations. Finally, decision logic resolves alternative routes. Traditional models often use fixed
branching probabilities, whereas data-aware approaches can condition route, time, or resource
choices on case context and the current process state [7, 9]. Manual modelling and simulation
discovery differ in how these elements are obtained, not in the need to represent them coher-
ently.
2.4.3 Decision Support and Model Calibration
Container terminal demand changes over time, resource capacity is limited, and operational
processes depend on each other. Managers make decisions about equipment, workforce, stor-
age, truck appointments, and capacity while the terminal remains active. T ests in the live ter-
minal can affect service quality and throughput. Simulation allows alternatives to be assessed
before they are implemented [34, 25, 26]. It is used to evaluate truck-appointment policies, plan
workforce and equipment, compare capacity investments, test yard-layout and storage strate-
gies, allocate cranes, identify congestion, and schedule maintenance.
Simulation supports what-if analysis by comparing scenarios under the same conditions.
Measures can include truck turnaround time, resource use, throughput, waiting time, and ser-
vice levels. This is useful in container terminals because a change in gate, yard, or vessel oper-
ations can affect other areas [4, 34, 25]. Truck-operation studies have assessed Truck Appoint-
ment Systems (TAS), gate capacity, yard use, and equipment allocation [6, 24].
A credible comparison requires calibration, which estimates or adjusts simulation parame-
ters to describe the observed operating conditions. T ypical tasks include fitting arrival and dura-

tion distributions, estimating resource capacities, and learning routing probabilities or context-
dependent decision rules from historical observations [7, 22]. In simulation discovery, many
of these steps form part of the automated discovery procedure itself. Calibration must still be

2.5 Simulation Discovery and Data-Aware Simulation
The previous sections introduced Business Process Simulation and event logs. Simulation dis-
covery connects both by estimating simulation behaviour from recorded process executions.
The estimated parameters are integrated into an executable process model [7, 22, 35]. Research
has moved from basic stochastic parameters towards context-dependent and data-aware be-
haviour.
From Simulation Discovery to Data-Driven Simulation Rozinat et al. proposed
an early approach for deriving simulation models from event logs [7]. It extracts control flow,
timing, resource assignments, arrivals, and routing probabilities from historical executions.
Later research extended this idea. Solti et al. [36] study stochastic Petri nets with flexible
delay distributions. Camargo et al. [22] proposed a framework that discovers process structure,
activity durations, resource pools, calendars, and arrival behaviour from event logs. Simod
further automates the discovery and calibration of simulation models [35].
Another research branch studies generative process models. Camargo et al. [37] compare
conventional data-driven simulation with deep-learning models. These models can learn de-
pendencies beyond separately fitted distributions, but they are harder to inspect and configure.
This issue is discussed in Section2.6.
Research has moved from extracting standard simulation parameters to learning relations
between process state and simulated behaviour [7, 22, 37].
A recent healthcare application makes the resulting methodological sequence explicit: an
event log is prepared, a process-simulation model is discovered, simulated and observed be-
haviour are compared, and only a sufficiently accurate model is used to examine alternative
resource configurations [14]. This order matters beyond healthcare. It separates evidence that
a model reproduces its historical operating regime from the later question of how it responds
to an intervention. The CTB study follows the same logic but adds the event-order, physical-
17

capacity, and logistics-specific validation required by a container-terminal setting.
The application also illustrates why automatic discovery is not necessarily the final model.
Domain experts modify the discovered structure where the Inductive-Miner abstraction is op-
erationally implausible and assess the simulation results for plausibility. Its authors identify
external delays, fine-grained inter-activity timing, drift, and structured stakeholder evaluation
as remaining limitations [14]. These are close to the validity boundaries investigated in CTB.
2.5.1 Adjacent Automatic Simulation Model Generation Research
Process-Mining-based simulation discovery is one route to reducing manual model construc-
tion, but it is not the only one. The adjacent Automatic Simulation Model Generation (ASMG)
literature studies how simulation structure and parameters can be derived from manufacturing
data, engineering models, sensor records, or system descriptions. Earlier and recent reviews treat
ASMG as a distinct research field motivated by the effort, error risk, and rapid obsolescence of
manually maintained material-flow simulations [18, 19]. This literature is important for the
CTB study because it addresses the same practical objective from a physical-system perspective,
whereas business-process simulation discovery often begins with a case-centric event log.
Lugaresi and Matta demonstrate that event data can support automatic discovery of both
manufacturing-system structure and parameters, but also show that the most detailed data-
derived model is not necessarily the most appropriate simulation abstraction. Their tuning step
selects a level of detail suited to the intended performance question [20]. Their later work uses
object-centric Process Mining to reconstruct systems with converging material flows, for which
a single case identifier loses relevant interaction structure [21]. These results reinforce a central
distinction in this thesis: automation of discovery does not establish the physical semantics or
purpose-validity of the resulting abstraction.
Object-centric Process Mining (OCPM) provides a related but distinct response to the lim-
itations of case-centric event data. Instead of assigning every event to one selected case, object-
centric event data allow an event to refer to multiple related objects and preserve their separate
identities, attributes, and relations [38]. In a container-terminal setting, a handling event could
therefore link a truck visit, truck, container, yard block, and handling resource rather than re-
ducing all of them to attributes of the truck-visit case. This representation can avoid conver-
gence and divergence distortions and retain the lifecycles through which the same container or
resource participates in multiple operations.
Initial work has extended this perspective from analysis towards executable simulation by
discovering object-centric process-simulation models [39]. At the same time, current discovery

research continues to address the interpretability of object-centric models. For example, Object-

Control-Flow and Stochastic Routing Early simulation-discovery approaches com-
bined a discovered process model with stochastic information from observed executions [7].
The process model defines which activities may occur, while stochastic weights estimate the
probability of alternative paths [36, 41].
The discovered control-flow structure defines the behaviour that the simulator can generate.
T oo much generalisation may allow implausible paths, while a restrictive model may miss valid

process variation [29, 42]. The balance between fitness and precision defines the behavioural
range of the simulator [42].
Vinci and de Leoni [42] make this link to simulation explicit in a four-step repair frame-
work. An initial stochastic Petri net is simulated, directly-follows features are used to train a
discriminator between real and simulated traces, and explainable feature importance identifies
behaviour that is systematically missing from or over-represented in the simulation. Three re-
pair strategies then add selected directly-follows relations while preserving soundness and safe-
ness: a complete basic repair, a smaller greedy repair, and an alignment-based repair that also
handles repeated activity labels. The repaired model is retained only when held-out fitness, pre-
cision and simulation-log distances improve. The framework therefore treats model repair as
a validated model comparison, not as an undocumented manual correction. Its currently op-
erationalised repairs add missing behaviour; restricting behaviour that is allowed too broadly
remains an open extension. This distinction matters when a discovered model is imprecise
rather than unfit. Across 23 evaluated source models, the harmonic fitness–precision score im-
proved in 19 cases; control-flow log distance fell in 16 cases, with a reported average reduction of
31.54 %. These results show that repair can materially improve simulation behaviour, but also
that no operator or acceptance rule dominates every model.
Temporal and Resource Behavior Early simulation-discovery approaches estimate exe-
cution times, waiting behaviour, and resource characteristics from event-log distributions [7,
36]. Later approaches model calendars, resource pools, allocation rules, and differences be-
tween resources [22, 43].
Timing often depends on operational context. Execution and waiting times may change
with workload, resource availability, case characteristics, or earlier process behaviour. Resource
selection may depend on more than a fixed activity-resource mapping [43, 13]. Recent ap-
proaches model these aspects conditionally instead of using one global distribution per activity
or resource.
Resource differentiation is not a cosmetic detail. In five synthetic and five real logs, López-
Pintado and Dumas report a mean cycle-time EMD of 4.63 for the fully differentiated setting,
compared with 9.45 for pooled undifferentiated resources and 13.42 for unpooled undifferenti-

accuracy, but it establishes that resource availability and individual performance can materially
change simulation fidelity.

Arrival Behaviour Classical simulation-discovery approaches estimate inter-arrival times
from historical case-start timestamps [7, 22]. Later approaches condition arrivals on time or

2.5.3 Data-Aware and Process-State-Aware Simulation
Classical stochastic simulation models often estimate one set of routing probabilities for all cases.
For example, if two paths occur with frequencies of 70 % and 30 %, the model may use these
probabilities for every case. This describes how often each path occurred, but not why cases
followed different paths [7, 41].
Data-aware simulation conditions stochastic behaviour on process data. Mannhardt et al.
[9] extend stochastic Petri nets with transition weights that depend on process variables. The
model can then capture differences between types of cases.
De Leoni et al. [10] extend this idea through the concept of theprocess state. Rather than
conditioning decisions only on static case attributes, the process state may also describe infor-
mation accumulated during the execution of the case, including process-variable values and the
history of previously executed activities. For every enabled transition, its conditional weight is
estimated from the state immediately before firing and normalised over all transitions enabled
in the current Petri net marking. Their two case studies show that a suitable state abstraction
can improve simulated control-flow frequencies relative to constant branching probabilities.
The best state reduced the reported control-flow distance by 7.5 % for the road-fines process
and 9.4 % for sepsis. The best abstraction is process-dependent, however: data alone, history,
and their combination do not dominate uniformly, and one history-based configuration pro-
duced a livelock. The study also repaired a permissive source model before simulation because
it allowed a one-activity execution absent from the log. Data-aware routing therefore comple-
ments, but does not replace, validation of the control-flow language itself.
The corresponding stochastic labelled data Petri-net formalism assigns state-dependent weights
to enabled transitions. It is deliberately not a full simulation formalism for every operational
aspect: transition choice can depend on state without representing spatial movement, resource
queues, or separate traffic delays. These mechanisms require additional timing and resource
models rather than more transition weights alone [10].
Conceptually, this research develops from fixed branching frequencies towards increasingly
conditional stochastic behaviour. Basic stochastic process models retain variation but apply
the same routing probabilities to every case. Data-aware models condition those probabilities
21

===== PDF PAGE 31 =====
on recorded attributes, while process-state-aware approaches additionally use information ac-
cumulated during the current execution. The stages are not mutually exclusive model classes;
each adds information that can explain why otherwise similar cases behave differently.
López-Pintado et al. [11] broaden the data representation through global, case, and event at-
tributes, attribute-update rules, and data-dependent branching conditions. In their evaluated
framework, however, attributes determine control flow; dependencies between data and activ-
ity start or duration remain future work. This boundary is important for physical logistics: a
data-aware routing formalism does not by itself represent movement or queueing time.
2.5.4 ProSiT as a Probabilistic Data-Aware Simulation Framework
ProSiT (PROcess SImulation T ool) applies data awareness to several simulation dimensions [13].
Its probabilistic white-box predictors model control-flow decisions, execution times, waiting
times, resource selection, and arrival behaviour.
The framework uses probabilistic decision trees to split the feature space. Each leaf contains
a local probability distribution. Simulation behaviour can vary by context while remaining
stochastic. This differs from both a model with one global distribution per parameter and a
deterministic model that returns one value.
ProSiT can use case-level attributes, execution history, resource information, temporal in-
formation, and workload features [13]. It does not support every event-level attribute that
changes during execution. The process-state approach of Mannhardt et al. [9] and de Leoni et
al. [10] covers a broader range of context than the current ProSiT implementation.
The published evaluation uses five case studies, an 80/20 temporal split, and five simulated
logs per model. ProSiT is competitive with a black-box baseline while keeping the rules in-
spectable [13]. The authors also draw a boundary essential for what-if use: the learned rela-
tionships capture correlations, not necessarily root causes. White-box inspection shows which
association the simulator uses; causal and operational validity still require domain and interven-
tion evidence.
ProSiT is useful for this thesis because its level of contextual conditioning can be changed.
This allows a comparison of model expressiveness and held-out fidelity in the CTB truck pro-
cess.
The framework’s expressiveness can be varied through two parameters that are central to

2.6 Explainable and White-Box Simulation
2.6.1 Explainability in Simulation-Based Decision Support
Business Process Simulation supports decisions about resources, process design, capacity, and
investments. Decision makers need to understand why a model produces an outcome and how
that outcome changes when parameters are modified. Traditional BPS models are transparent
because their control flow, routing probabilities, resources, and timing assumptions are defined
by hand [33].
Machine-learning models can learn behaviour from historical event data and may improve
prediction [22, 37]. However, their internal logic can be difficult to inspect. A model may re-
produce historical behaviour without showing why it selected a route, waiting time, or resource.
This makes validation and what-if analysis harder [12]. For what-if analysis, users must be able
to change parameters or rules and judge whether the model response is reasonable. Explainable
simulation tries to combine data-driven prediction with visible decision logic.
2.6.2 Probabilistic White-Box Simulation Models
Probabilistic white-box models make learned decisions visible without removing the variation
required for simulation. The RIMS framework, for example, combines discrete-event simu-
lation with predictive models evaluated at runtime while retaining an inspectable simulation
structure [12].
ProSiT applies this principle to the simulation dimensions introduced in Section2.5.4. Each
decision tree routes the current simulated state through visible conditions to a leaf containing
a probability distribution. The resulting draw remains stochastic, but the reason that a partic-
ular local distribution was selected can be inspected. Users can also change the conditions or
distributions directly instead of retraining an opaque deep-learning model [13].
Published evaluations show that probabilistic white-box models can reach similar accuracy
23

===== PDF PAGE 33 =====
to black-box alternatives while being easier to inspect and configure [13]. However, the evi-
dence mainly comes from benchmarks and business-process settings. There is little evidence
from physical logistics systems such as container terminals. Explainability is difficult to mea-
sure. A decision tree is easier to read than a neural network, but this does not prove that its
rules are correct or useful. White-box simulation must balance accuracy, explainability, and
configurability.
For terminological precision, the relationships exposed by such learned rules are statistical
associations conditional on the training data and model specification. Their visibility supports
inspection and deliberate configuration, but does not by itself establish causal effects.
2.7 Simulation Verification, V alidation, and Trust-
worthiness
V alidation asks whether a simulator is accurate enough for its intended use, not only whether
it runs. A model may reproduce historical truck turnaround distributions but still fail to pre-
dict an intervention outside the range of the observed data [2, 33]. Automatically discovered
simulation models do not remove this requirement. They replace some manual assumptions
with assumptions about event-log semantics, discovery algorithms, data attributes and fitted
distributions, all of which require examination.
2.7.1 Distinction Between Verification and V alidation

Finally,operational validityasks whether the computerised model’s output behaviour is ac-

validation—running the model under historical input conditions and comparing its output
with observed system data—is one technique for assessing operational validity [2].

2.7.2 From Historical Logs to Operational V alidation
Process Mining provides a natural representation for historical-data validation because both
the observed system and the simulator can be represented by event logs. Rozinat et al. estab-
lished an early version of this principle: they discover control-flow, data, performance and re-
source perspectives, integrate them into a coloured Petri-net simulation model, simulate a sec-
ond event log, and analyse how well that generated log approximates the characteristics of the
25

===== PDF PAGE 35 =====
original log [7]. This is stronger than checking whether the discovered model can execute; it
evaluates whether its generated behaviour resembles recorded behaviour.
Camargo et al. formalise the same idea as an optimisation objective. Candidate BPS config-
urations generate event logs and hyperparameter optimisation searches for the configuration
that maximises similarity to observed log behaviour [22]. Their later comparison of data-driven
simulation and deep generative models further shows why quality must not be inferred from
case-sequence frequencies alone: conventional data-driven simulators may reproduce activity
sequences and their frequencies while performing worse on temporal dynamics [37].
The implication is that operational fidelity is multi-dimensional. Relevant perspectives in-
clude control flow, activity and case timing, case arrivals, congestion and resource behaviour.
A model can be close to the real log in one perspective and far away in another; collapsing all
discrepancies into one number can hide the mechanism that is incorrectly represented [44, 45].
2.7.3 Multi-Perspective Event-Log Comparison
Chapela-Campa et al. provide a validation framework that matches the evaluation problem of
this thesis [44, 45]. Their starting point is that the true BPS model is normally unavailable and
that different simulation engines use incomparable internal formats. They evaluate a model
indirectly as a generator: multiple logs generated by the BPS model are compared with an ac-
tual event log that was not used to construct the model. The framework separates control-flow,
temporal and congestion perspectives and reports a measure for each instead of using one aggre-
gate score for overall model quality. The measures are evaluated using controlled perturbations
and by comparing alternative automatically discovered BPS models, showing that perspective-
specific measures can identify the source of a discrepancy [45].
From the control-flow perspective, output quality asks whether the simulator generates ac-
tivity sequences and sequence frequencies resembling the observed process. It is distinct from
replay fitness of a control-flow model: fitness asks whether real case sequences are allowed by
the model, whereas output validation asks which sequences the stochastic simulator actually
generates and with what frequency.
Stochastic conformance work applies transport distances to probability distributions over
case sequences or other behavioural abstractions [46]. Chapela-Campa et al. instead define two
simulation-oriented log distances [44, 45]. Control-Flow Log Distance (CFLD) computes the
normalised Damerau–Levenshtein distance between pairs of case sequences and uses the Hun-
garian algorithm to find the minimum-cost one-to-one matching between the two logs. CFLD
compares complete case sequences; it is not merely a comparison of variant-frequency vectors.
26

===== PDF PAGE 36 =====
Because this matching is computationally expensive, N-Gram Distance (NGD) compares nor-
malised frequencies of local activity subsequences. NGD is scalable and detects local ordering
differences, although it loses some full-case information.
This thesis does not claim to implement the full Chapela-Campa metric suite. Its structural
evidence combines held-out Petri-net conformance, explicit case-language contracts and yard-
activity frequency error. These measures answer the CTB-specific structural questions, while
the Chapela-Campa framework supplies the rationale for keeping structural evidence separate
from temporal fidelity.
Identical or similar case sequences do not imply similar process performance. From the tem-
poral perspective, validation considers whether the simulator reproduces when events occur,
how cases progress after arrival and how long activities and complete cases take. Camargo et
al. show empirically that a generative model may reproduce control-flow frequencies but still
miss temporal dynamics [37].
The Chapela-Campa framework distinguishes three event-distribution measures [44, 45].
Absolute Event Distribution(AED) compares event counts across absolute calendar-time bins;
Circadian Event Distribution(CED) compares recurring weekday and hour-of-day patterns;
andRelative Event Distribution(RED) offsets timestamps by each case’s arrival and compares
its temporal progression. AED and RED meanevent distribution, not absolute or relative error
distance. For end-to-end performance, Cycle Time Distribution (CTD) compares the empiri-
cal distributions of complete case durations.
The CTB evaluation uses more directly interpretable distributions at the level of its research
questions: inter-arrival time, per-activity yard service time, model-derived pre-service delay and
case turnaround time. Reporting complete distributions rather than only their means is use-
ful because equal averages can conceal differences in dispersion, skewness or tails. Per-activity
results prevent common RMG events from concealing weaker fidelity for less frequent yard
activities.
The arrival and congestion perspectives address system-level consequences of the interaction
between demand, service requirements and resource availability. These consequences cannot
be validated from activity-duration marginals alone. Chapela-Campa et al. use Case Arrival
Rate (CAR) to compare binned arrival counts and shapes, and CTD to compare another con-
gestion component, the length of time cases remain in the system [44, 45]. An arrival model
can be temporally unrealistic even if it generates the correct total number of cases.
This issue matters at CTB: truck demand determines competition for yard resources, and
simultaneous arrivals result from the minute-resolved source data. The evaluation reports inter-

Additional Requirements for Data-Aware Discovery A data-aware simulator con-
ditions behaviour on case attributes, process state or workload information rather than using
only global distributions and branching frequencies [10, 11, 13]. This adds a validation layer.
First, every predictor used at a simulated decision point must be available at that point; at-
tributes computed from a later outcome create target leakage even if aggregate log similarity is
high. Second, discovery and model selection must be separated from the final test log. Third,
the generated log must satisfy process and domain invariants that aggregate distances may over-
look.
White-box rules help because their split conditions and leaf distributions can be inspected
and modified [13]. Interpretability, however, is not equivalent to validity or causality. A read-

duce the same marginal distribution. V alidation should combine held-out output fidelity with
rule inspection, leakage checks, structural contracts, domain constraints and sensitivity analy-
sis. This matters in the CTB application because timestamp fields are operational proxies and
a resource label does not always denote a physically interchangeable crane or storage position.

2.7.4 Trustworthiness, Limitations, and Research Gap
Historical fidelity is necessary for a simulator that claims to represent an existing process, but
it is not sufficient for unrestricted decision support. Chapela-Campa et al. identify this limit:
comparison with an original event log assesses replication of theas-isprocess, not the accuracy
of predictions after a process change [44, 45]. Similarly, Sargent’s purpose-relative view implies
that evidence collected in one operating region cannot automatically validate use in another
[2].
Trustworthiness cannot be measured with one metric. Evidence is needed for data and con-
ceptual validity, implementation, held-out fidelity, reproducibility, assumptions, stochastic ro-
bustness, domain review, and the limits of extrapolation. ProSiT provides probabilistic rules
that can be inspected and configured [13]. Applied work shows how a discovered simulation
model can be connected to a resource-allocation problem [14]. These properties support vali-
dation and scenario analysis, but they do not replace evidence for each intervention.
This thesis does not claim to solve the general trustworthiness problem of simulation discov-
ery. It contributes a container-terminal case study: the model is tested against an unseen histor-
ical period, across structural, temporal and arrival perspectives, with repeated stochastic runs
and explicit domain constraints. Scenario results are interpreted as conditional consequences
of the documented model assumptions, not as experimentally established causal effects.
Research Gap Three literature strands are relevant to this setting. Process-mining-based
simulation research contributes discovery, conformance, and validation methods for deriving
executable models from event logs; ASMG and related manufacturing studies show how physical-
system structure and material-flow dependencies can be represented in formal abstractions; and
logistics-oriented Process Mining studies highlight the need to account for operational context,
queues, and resource interactions beyond timestamps alone. The present gap is therefore not
simply that one method is missing, but that the literature reviewed for this thesis does not yet
provide a temporally validated, data-aware executable model for a container-terminal operation
that is also checked against physical resource constraints and what-if validity conditions.
Container terminals are dynamic logistics systems in which truck, yard, storage, and equip-
ment processes interact under limited resource capacities. Simulation is therefore widely used
to support operational and tactical decision making in this environment [34, 25, 26]. Most
30

===== PDF PAGE 40 =====
existing terminal simulation models, however, are developed manually, which requires sub-
stantial modelling effort, calibration, maintenance, and domain knowledge [7]. Simulation-
discovery research offers an alternative by deriving simulation models and parameters directly
from event logs. Recent approaches increasingly incorporate data-aware decision logic, contex-
tual information, process state, and automated model construction [22, 35, 9, 10, 13]. ProSiT ex-
tends this development through probabilistic white-box models whose decision logic remains
inspectable and configurable [13]. Despite these advances, empirical evaluations have largely fo-
cused on healthcare, administration, and conventional business processes, while evidence from
large-scale physical logistics environments such as container terminals remains limited. It is
therefore still unclear how robustly automatically derived simulation models transfer to settings
characterised by congestion, shared physical resources, spatial dependencies, and changing op-
erational workloads, and to what extent such models can support credible what-if analysis and
decision support. This thesis addresses this gap through a case study of truck-handling opera-
tions at HHLA Container T erminal Burchardkai (CTB), evaluating both the strengths and the
limitations of data-aware simulation discovery in this setting.

3.2.3 Case and Activity Definition
A casein the event log corresponds to a single truck visit, uniquely identified byFAHRPLAN_UID.
Each case represents the complete sequence of activities executed by one truck between gate
entry and gate exit.
The data-aware simulation framework used in this thesis [13] accepts only case-level contex-
tual attributes. T o attach block-specific context features (target_utilization, target_demand,
target_rank) consistently, the design decision was made to include only process instances
that interact with exactly one RMG block. Visits involving multiple RMG blocks are excluded
because a single value could not be assigned to their block-specific congestion measures. Visits
to other yard areas (VC or LL) that involve exactly one RMG block are retained. The filtering
logic is implemented infilter_one_rmg_only.py.
This case-centric design does not discard the container characteristics that are available for
a retained truck visit; they remain available as contextual predictors. It does, however, collapse
the independent lifecycles and relations of trucks, containers, yard blocks, and equipment into
one visit-oriented record. An object-centric event log could preserve these entities and associate
a handling event with all participating objects [38]. It could also retain multi-block visits with-
out forcing one block-level value onto the complete case. Such a representation would require
a different source-data mapping and an object-centric simulation engine [39]; it is therefore
outside the scope of the ProSiT-based experiment rather than an untested variant of the same
input configuration.

4.1 Goals of the Experiments
The experiments assess whether a data-aware process simulation model can be constructed
from historical CTB truck-operation data and used as a credible representation of the observed
process. The aim is not to optimise the terminal or to show that a more complex model is
automatically better. The aim is to test what the discovered model reproduces, where the dis-
covery workflow requires domain intervention, and what can be learned from its responses to
controlled operational changes.
The first goal is to construct an executable simulation model from the event log while pre-
serving the physical sequence of a truck visit. The expected workflow starts with automatic
process discovery. Its output is checked not only through process-mining quality measures
but also against the case contract of one Gate In, sequential yard handling, and one final Gate
Out. If the discovered structure violates this contract, the experiment must make the required
correction explicit instead of treating a formally valid model as an operationally valid simula-
tion.
The second goal is to measure how well the resulting simulation reproduces an unseen pe-
riod of terminal operation. This includes the activity sequence, service times, truck turnaround
times, and the arrival process. Three ProSiT configurations are compared to test whether addi-
tional routing and workload context improves held-out fidelity. This is tested directly. More
context is treated as a modelling choice, not as an expected improvement.
The third goal is to examine the model as a white-box tool for operational what-if analysis.
The selected model is exposed to a resource-pool change at T22 and to a higher truck-arrival
rate. These interventions test whether the specified input changes reach the simulation and
whether the resulting KPI changes are interpretable within the model boundaries. They do
not establish that the same effects would occur in the real terminal.
Across these goals, robustness is assessed through a temporal hold-out, repeated simulation
runs, model-quality diagnostics, and sensitivity checks. T ogether, these experiments provide
the evidence required to answer the research questions introduced in Chapter1.
4.1.1 Layered V alidity Audit
The experimental design treats simulation validity as a sequence of distinct claims. First, the
event semantics must preserve the recorded order and case boundaries. Second, the process
model must replay held-out behaviour without violating the sequential truck contract. Third,
the stochastic model must reproduce held-out routing, timing, arrivals, and case outcomes.
47

===== PDF PAGE 57 =====
Fourth, the transfer from the discovery period to the later period must be separated from cali-
bration error. Fifth, resource and location labels must be checked against their physical mean-
ing. Only after these layers does scenario response become evidence relevant to counterfactual
decision support. This ordering follows the multi-perspective principle that apparently strong
aggregate fit can hide perspective-specific errors [45, 2].
The layers are complementary rather than alternative metrics. A failure at a later layer does
not invalidate every earlier result; it limits the conclusion that can be drawn. For example, a net
may be structurally sound and replay the hold-out while its simulator still omits physical tran-
sition time. Likewise, a parameter can be changed reproducibly without the model containing
the mechanism through which the real terminal would react. The results chapter therefore re-
ports both the successful layer and the first unsupported stronger claim instead of assigning
one global validity score.
4.2 Solution Architecture
The solution architecture is organised as a sequence of persistent processing blocks. Each block
produces an artefact that is used by the next block. This makes the split, model discovery, sim-
ulation, and evaluation reproducible and prevents information from the test period from en-
tering model discovery. Figure4.1gives an overview of the complete pipeline.
The input is the simulation-ready event log from Chapter3. It is divided chronologically
into a training partition and an unseen test partition. Only the training data are used to dis-
cover the control flow and the ProSiT simulation parameters. The training log is loaded in its
explicit XES event order and an Inductive-Miner model is discovered. A hard structural check
rejects any model containing a parallel operator because the activities of one truck must be se-
quential. The accepted Petri net becomes the common control-flow skeleton for all simulation
configurations.
ProSiT then discovers arrival, timing, routing, and resource parameters from the training
partition. The three configurations differ in the amount of context made available to their
decision models. Each discovered parameter bundle is stored separately and simulated over the
same time window and case volume as the test partition. Repeated runs with different seeds
produce the simulation logs used for the fidelity comparison and confidence intervals.
The evaluation block compares the simulated logs with the held-out event log from sev-
eral perspectives. It checks control-flow coverage, timing distributions, point statistics, arrival
behaviour, and resource assumptions. After this historical evaluation, one transparent param-

4.4 Process Discovery
4.4.1 Discovery Input and Event-Order Semantics
In a data-aware process-simulation workflow, control flow is discovered before the timing, rout-
ing, arrival, and resource parameters are fitted. For ProSiT, the resulting Petri net is not merely
a diagram: it is the executable backbone that determines which transition can fire next. The
selected model therefore has to balance fitness, precision, generalisation, and simplicity while
also respecting the physical meaning of a truck visit [30, 29].
The CTB domain restriction is deliberately narrow. Within one case,Gate In must pre-
cede the yard work andGate Out must follow it; two activities of the same truck may not
execute in parallel. Different trucks may still be processed concurrently through the simula-
tor’s resource capacities. The order among yard activities is not constrained because it depends
on the handling requirements of the visit and repetitions are operationally possible.
An initial diagnostic conversion through a PM4Py DataFrame formatter sorted events by
timestamps. Because CTB start and completion timestamps can overlap at minute resolution,
this conversion did not necessarily preserve the explicit event order stored in the XES traces
and produced a model containing parallel cuts. That model was an artefact of the import path,
not the final discovery result. The authoritative pipeline now reads the XES file directly as a
PM4Py event log, preserving the recorded within-trace order. The original timestamps remain
unchanged for all time and resource models. This distinction is methodologically important:
activity order is part of the event-log semantics and must not be reconstructed implicitly from
coarse or overlapping timestamps.

4.4.3 Held-Out Conformance and Case-Contract V alidation
The discovered net is evaluated on the training and test partitions through token-based replay
fitness and precision, together with PM4Py’sgeneralization_tbr andsimplicity_petri_net
measures. Fitness tests whether observed traces can be replayed; precision penalises behaviour
allowed by the model but not supported by the log; generalisation rewards an abstraction that
is not tied to individual observations; and simplicity describes structural economy. No single
metric establishes model suitability. Their joint interpretation is combined with the explicit
structural check that counts parallel operators and with simulation-output contracts.
The generated logs are also checked against the sequential case contract. Each case must con-
tain one initialGate In and one finalGate Out ; activities inside one case may not overlap,
Gate Out may not precede yard work, and completion timestamps may not decrease. Gate-
only paths remain a reported overgeneralisation diagnostic because they are explicitly permit-
ted by the unchanged discovered net; they are not conflated with parallel execution. This
separates replay coverage from physical sequence validity. The protocol is implemented in
baseline/07_run_prosit_discovery.py and_eventlog_contract.py. Its results
are reported in Section5.2.

4.6 Monte-Carlo Replication Protocol
Every ProSiT simulation includes stochastic draws from the fitted distributions. One Monte-
Carlo draw is not enough to conclude that one configuration is more faithful than another:
the observed difference may be within the sampling variance of the simulator itself. This is
the same many-possible-realisations problem addressed by the multi-log validation design of
Chapela-Campa et al. and by classical independent-replication output analysis [44, 48]. This
thesis reports every fidelity metric with a Monte-Carlo confidence interval.

their mean contribution, and are accompanied by median and p90 values. This prevents a rare
but long event from being called the dominant bottleneck solely because of its mean.

4.8 What-If Scenario Design
The operational value of a logistics simulation lies in comparing future decisions under con-
ditions that cannot safely be tested in the live terminal. The intended workflow begins with
a forecast operating condition, compares candidate policies through simulated process execu-
tions, and evaluates their activity- and system-level consequences. A forecast of high yard utili-
sation could, for example, motivate a reduction in truck slots released per hour or a temporary
policy that favours retrieval-related handling. A useful model must then show not only to-
tal turnaround but also whether receive and delivery service or pre-service delay change differ-
ently. These disaggregated effects are essential: an acceptable terminal-wide average can conceal
a harmful shift for one handling pattern.
T wo independent interventions are evaluated to address RQ4. Both are derived by deep
copy from the frozen, training-only calibratedrules+workloadbaseline. No held-out observa-
tion is used to select or fit a scenario parameter. The experiments are deliberately simple tests
of this decision-support chain, rather than proposed operating policies.
4.8.1 Scenario A: T22 Resource-Pool Reallocation
Scenario A removes T22 from the simulated RMG resource pool. The scenario is constructed
by (i) removing T22 from everyparams.act_to_resources entry that references it, after
verifying that each affected activity retains at least one eligible resource; and (ii) setting all 168
weekly calendar slots of T22 to unavailable. All other parameters are unchanged.
This is a resource-pool reallocation experiment, not a complete spatial yard-closure model.
The discovered selector may use target-area attributes as statistical predictors, but ProSiT does
not impose a hard constraint that binds a container to its historical storage block. A truck
whose historical target was T22 can be assigned to another eligible block without an explicit

4.8.2 Scenario B: 20 % Higher Truck Demand
Scenario B changes only the empirical working-time inter-arrival distribution. For demand
multiplier m = 1.20, every empirical inter-arrival sample xi is replaced by xi/m. This preserves
the zero mass and the empirical distribution shape while increasing the modelled arrival inten-

resource mappings, resource calendars and case-attribute schema remain unchanged. The sce-
nario is independent of the T22 resource-pool reallocation. Operationally, the admitted truck
rate is a quantity a terminal can influence in advance through the number and timing of ap-

tion decisions, this scenario represents a change in the effective admitted arrival intensity; it is
not a detailed model of a particular appointment-system policy.

4.10 ProSiT Implementation Notes and Workarounds
Applying ProSiT 1.0.3 [13] to the CTB event log uncovered several implementation issues that
would have propagated as silent misbehaviour if not addressed. This section documents them
so that the experiments can be reproduced deterministically and so that the downstream fidelity
numbers can be interpreted correctly.
Data-Attribute Discovery on NaN Columns The defaultattribute_mode='distribution'
passes each case attribute through a per-attribute distribution fitter. In the CTB log,full_ratio
andis_full containNaN for cases that involve empty-container-only visits. The distribu-
tion fitter in ProSiT 1.0.3 forwards the raw values toscipy.stats, which raisesTypeError:
ufunc 'isfinite' not supported on the mixed dtype. Switching toattribute_mode='empirical'
64

===== PDF PAGE 74 =====
bypasses the per-attribute fitter and samples full attribute tuples from the observed joint dis-
tribution instead. This is not a fidelity compromise: the discovered simulator still preserves
case-level attribute correlations, and empirical sampling from the training distribution is the
appropriate choice given the number of categorical attributes present in the CTB log.
Simulation in No-Rules Mode In no-rulesmode ProSiT samples the routing choice via
random.choice(seq) whereseq is anumpy.ndarray. Python’s built-inrandom.choice
evaluatesif not seq: , which raisesValueError: The truth value of an array with
more than one element is ambiguous. on multi-element numpy arrays. The workaround
is a minimal monkey patch that interceptsrandom.choice while the engine is running and
forwardsnumpy.ndarray arguments throughnumpy.random.randint. The patch is con-
tained to the simulation call and is restored immediately afterwards. It is applied by both
baseline/07_run_prosit_discovery.py andvalidation/06_multi_seed_ci.py.
Duplicated Columns in the Simulated Log ProSiT 1.0.3 writes case-level attributes
into the event-level frame returned bySimulatorEngine.apply(). When a case-level at-
tribute has the same name as an event attribute (in the CTB log this happens forenabled:timestamp
because ProSiT writes case-level attributes into the event-level frame), the resultingpandas.DataFrame
carries duplicate column labels. Standard operations such aspd.to_datetime(df[col])
then return aDataFrame slice instead of aSeries and fail. The workaround is a singledf.loc[:,
~df.columns.duplicated()] call at the start of every KPI-computation function. It is ap-
plied both in the what-if scripts and in the multi-seed CI driver.
Temporal Features Outside the ProSiT Input The final discovery contract does not
pass hour-of-day or day-of-week attributes to ProSiT. It keeps the five standard event-log columns
and the approved visit-related, demand, utilisation and target attributes from T able3.8. Other
columns are removed before simulation-parameter discovery. Hour and weekday are derived
from timestamps only in the separate arrival and explanatory analyses. The retained ProSiT
configurations therefore do not condition their timing rules directly on calendar features. This
is an input-model boundary when interpreting the day/night differences reported in Section5.6.
Non-Deterministic Simulation Engine SimulatorEngine.apply() does not accept
a seed argument. The Monte-Carlo protocol of Section4.6 seeds Python’srandom andnumpy.random
before every call toapply(). Seeding alone is insufficient across Python processes, however.
ProSiT obtains enabled transitions from a set, while PM4Py hashes transition objects by in-
memory identity. After a model is reloaded, the same enabled transitions can therefore be
presented to the weighted sampler in a different order and consume the same random draw

differently. The reviewer runner canonicalises this list by the stable Petri-net transition name
immediately before the existing weighted draw. It changes neither the net nor the learned tran-
sition weights. Independent fresh-process runs with the same seed then produce identical re-

guarantee used for the scenario results in Chapter5.

The observed process changes between discovery and evaluation. Mean truck turnaround rises

(95 % W elch CI[2.003, 2.896]). The median rises from 29 to 32 minutes and P90 from 67 to
71 minutes; the bootstrapped 95 % interval for the P90 increase is[3, 6] minutes. The W asser-

therefore not an exchangeable sample from an unchanged period.
The change is not confined to case composition. Mean service time increases for all nine
yard activities, with activity-specific increases between 1.20 and 4.01 minutes. Using held-out

a stable causal measurement of terminal congestion. They remain predictive features whose

with recent work on online simulation discovery, which gives greater weight to recent data while
retaining historical information to handle evolving processes [50].

5.9 Held-Out Bottleneck Analysis
A bottleneck analysis must distinguish a long individual activity from a frequent activity at
which a large amount of system time accumulates. The held-out log is therefore decomposed
at event level. Recorded handling service is completion minus start. The non-negative gap
from the previous event’s completion to the current event’s start is calledcomposite pre-service
delay. For the sequential truck process, the sum of both components exactly reconstructs each
case turnaround. Composite pre-service delay can contain movement, traffic and transfer, dis-
patching, and queueing; it is not interpreted as a direct queue measurement.
Across the 17,892 held-out visits, mean turnaround is 39.742 minutes. Recorded handling
contributes 14.755 minutes per visit and composite pre-service delay contributes 24.987 minutes.
The decomposition error is zero for every case. T able5.12ranks activities by their frequency-
weighted contribution rather than their mean duration alone.
The largest component is the gap before Gate Out: 11.273 minutes per visit on average
and 201,690 minutes in total. It contains the outbound transition and any unobserved re-
lease or exit delay, so calling Gate Out a queueing bottleneck would be too strong. RMG
receive is the largest handling-related accumulation point. Its mean service is 13.779 minutes
and its mean composite pre-service delay is 13.974 minutes; together the activity accounts for
27.99 % of all reconstructed held-out time. RMG delivery accounts for a further 15.45 %. These

5.11 Interpretation: From Process Mining to Data-Aware
Simulation
The results become most informative when the structural, temporal and scenario findings are
interpreted together. They do not describe one model that is simply “good” or “bad.” Instead,
they show that different forms of process-mining evidence answer different questions. The
compact Inductive-Miner net reproduces the held-out control flow while generalising across
sequential yard combinations, the arrival model reproduces an important part of the workload
timing, and the simulator remains too fast at the level of complete truck visits. These findings
are compatible because control-flow conformance, arrival fidelity, and end-to-end temporal fi-
delity are distinct properties [37, 44, 45].
Meaning for traditional Process Mining Traditional Process Mining is strongest in
this study as a language for reconstructing and checking observed process behaviour. Here, its
most important diagnostic is not merely the near-perfect held-out fitness. It is the discovery
that the apparent parallelism vanished when the explicit XES event order was preserved. The
earlier parallel model represented a timestamp-based reordering artefact; the final process tree
has no parallel operator. This demonstrates a classical Process-Mining point with direct simu-
lation consequences: the event-log ordering relation is part of the model input, and a formally
sound discovery can still encode the wrong semantics when coarse timestamps are treated as an
unambiguous order [7, 29].
The final model makes the fitness–precision–generalisation trade-off visible. It fits 99.994 %
of held-out traces and retains a held-out generalisation of 0.9413, including behaviour not stored
as an exact training path. Precision is lower at 0.7558 because the loop admits additional yard
combinations and repetitions. In this case, that broader language is not equivalent to physical
parallelism and is not automatically an error: the terminal process allows varying sequential ac-
tivity orders. The domain check must therefore be semantic and operator-specific. Penalising
every unseen order would collapse discovery into memorisation, while accepting a parallel cut
would violate the truck-visit contract.
The silent gate-only path makes the same trade-off concrete. It is permitted by the discov-
ered language and retained as an auditable finding rather than removed through an undocu-
mented guard. Its rarity in simulation—0.059 % forno-rulesand zero cases forrules+workload—
shows why formal language analysis and realised-output validation answer complementary ques-
tions. A permitted path need not be common, yet it remains relevant when the model is inter-

===== PDF PAGE 97 =====
preted beyond the observed period.
This is a precision-side model-repair problem. The simulation-guided framework of Vinci
and de Leoni [42] provides a rigorous way to identify differences between real and simulated
control flow and to compare an original and repaired net on held-out data. Its published oper-
ators currently add missing behaviour, however, whereas the CTB bypass requires restricting
excess behaviour. De Leoni et al. [10] demonstrate that an explicit manual restriction before
simulation can be defensible when a source model permits a minimal trace absent from the log.
For CTB, such a restricted net should therefore be evaluated as a separate robustness model,
not substituted silently for the discovered model. The rare realised frequency does not by itself
make the formal defect harmless.
The same separation applies to time. Good control-flow coverage does not account for the
8.170-minute mean turnaround deficit or the 17.310-minute P90 deficit. The simulator can put
activities in the correct order and still omit time between them. In the CTB setting, this miss-
ing time may contain movement, traffic, resource interaction, queueing or operational states
that are not represented as events. The temporal bias is therefore not a contradiction of the
conformance result; it identifies a different abstraction boundary. This is why evaluating an
event-log-derived simulator only through replay fitness or variant frequencies would have pro-
duced an overly positive conclusion.
The transition-baseline diagnostic makes the scale of that abstraction boundary concrete.
Route-specific p5 values lie between 2.16 and 5.28 minutes, and the frequency-weighted in-
bound and outbound lower-tail components sum to 7.62 minutes per visit. Their proximity to
the 8.170-minute mean deficit is consistent with omitted physical movement being a major con-
tributor. It does not prove that all residual time is travel, because the baseline also contains un-
separated traffic and operational delay and because a percentile summary cannot be subtracted
case by case. The fair conclusion is therefore stronger than merely saying that “some time is
missing,” but weaker than claiming a validated travel-time mechanism.
Meaning for data-aware Process Mining Recent data-aware methods extend conven-
tional discovery by conditioning routing, duration or resource behaviour on case attributes
and process state [9, 10, 13]. The CTB results show both the value and the limit of that exten-
sion. Its value is selective rather than universal. Calendar context contains a strong and stable
signal for arrivals: the time-aware benchmark reduces arrival-count MAE by 67.5 % and RMSE
by 51.2 %, and the day arrival rate is about 8.7 times the night rate. In this part of the process,
conditioning on information available at case creation captures a real predictive regularity.
The duration results point in the other direction. For the two dominant RMG activities, the

===== PDF PAGE 98 =====
diagnostic decision trees have slightly negative held-out R2. The strict ablation makes the source
of the endpoint result clearer. Process- and case-state rules improve turnaround EMD and activ-
ity incidence, but adding workload information afterward leaves weighted service-time EMD
unchanged within a wide paired interval and worsens turnaround and routing measures. These
observations are consistent with limited stable duration signal: conditional branches may be in-
terpretable without adding useful later-period predictive information. They are not, however,
a formal causal chain or proof that workload never matters. They show that the available CTB
workload representation does not transfer as useful state in this experiment.
This qualification is central to the interpretation of data awareness. Adding attributes in-
creases representational capacity, but useful capacity depends on information timing, measure-
ment quality and a relationship that is stable across the training and application regimes. Case-
level target demand or utilisation may be correlated with an observed duration without measur-
ing the physical mechanism that caused it. Conversely, a weak marginal relationship can hide
omitted event-level states such as the actual queue, truck position or equipment movement.
The white-box character of ProSiT is valuable here because the learned conditions can be in-
spected and challenged; interpretability makes the association auditable, but does not convert
it into causality or guarantee predictive validity [13, 2].
This result sharpens rather than contradicts the state-abstraction findings of Vinci et al. [10].
Their best-performing abstraction varies between the two evaluated processes, and an unsuit-
able history state can create problematic simulated behaviour. The CTB ablation supplies the
missing application-specific test: case and process state help selected routing measures, whereas
the available workload state does not improve the dominant timing error. It also goes beyond
data-aware routing alone. The framework of López-Pintado et al. [11] demonstrates the value
of case data for control-flow decisions but identifies data-dependent activity duration as a sep-
arate extension. CTB shows why that separation matters in physical logistics: state-dependent
firing probabilities cannot by themselves create travel, queues, or spatially constrained service.
The resource result is equally consistent with the differentiated-resource evidence of López-
Pintado and Dumas [43]. Treating resources as statistically interchangeable can preserve event
counts while distorting timing. In CTB, capping nominal RMG concurrency does not make
target assignment spatially sticky and does not model crane or truck movement. By contrast,
physical container-terminal simulation represents travel, equipment interaction and allocation
policies explicitly [25]. The current hybrid research opportunity is therefore to retain the dis-
covered white-box process and state rules while supplying a mechanistic movement, queue, and
resource-assignment layer.

Correlation, scenarios, and operational meaning The results therefore distinguish
predictive association from intervention response. The temporal arrival features show a strong
association with when trucks enter the terminal, but the 20 % arrival intervention produces no
resolved mean-turnaround increase. This combination does not imply that real CTB turnaround
is independent of demand. It shows that the fitted simulator contains an arrival relationship,
but the chosen change remains below mean block saturation and the model lacks enough congestion-
generating state and interaction to translate short-period pressure into end-to-end delay. In the
same way, eliminating T22 assignments verifies that the resource rule was changed, while the
near-zero turnaround effect reflects reallocation within an abstract interchangeable resource
pool. It is not evidence that closing a physical block would have no operational effect.
T aken together, the scenario results act as stress tests of the model’s semantics. A null ef-
fect can be a substantive operational finding only when the mechanisms through which the
intervention should propagate are represented and validated. Here, the null responses instead
expose missing spatial state, location feasibility and explicit queue dynamics, while the offered-
load audit also shows that a stronger, explicitly saturation-targeted intervention would be needed
to isolate congestion response. The contribution of the data-aware white-box model is conse-
quently twofold: it supports reproducible and inspectable changes, and it makes the boundary
between an implemented association and a credible physical mechanism visible. That bound-
ary is the main result for decision support. The model is suitable for analysing conditional con-
sequences inside its documented abstraction, but not yet for claiming causal terminal effects
from the two scenarios.
The target application nevertheless remains concrete. Before publishing truck appointments,
a terminal could combine a utilisation forecast with candidate hourly slot limits, simulate each
policy, and compare turnaround together with receive- and delivery-specific service and pre-
service KPIs. If forecast conditions threaten retrieval performance, a candidate policy could
reduce the admitted truck rate or prioritise retrieval-compatible resources; the simulator would
quantify both the intended benefit and any transferred delay to delivery operations. This prospec-
tive comparison is the benefit that a validated logistics simulation should provide. The CTB ex-
periments establish the transparent experimental interface and show exactly which additional
queue, location, and equipment states must be represented before that interface can be used
prescriptively.

5.13 Methodological Limitations
Pre-Service Delay ProSiT enables an activity when the process model allows it. Consecu-
tive CTB activities can occur at different locations, but truck movement is not a separate activity
in the event-log design. The time from completion of one activity to the start of the next can
therefore combine
Dpre-service≈ Tphysical+ Ttraffic+ Tqueue+ Tother. (5.3)
The log cannot identify these parts separately. The percentile check shows that a reconstructed
baseline is sensitive to its assumption. It does not measure any one part. This would require
position, trajectory, queue-entry, or similar state data. These data are not available. The results
use the termpre-service delay, not queueing time.
Resource Assignment Is Not Spatial Container Relocation An RMG resource
in ProSiT is an abstract capacity assignment. The discovered overlap capacity is 100. The stated
physical cap is 66 across 22 blocks. This mismatch shows that timestamp overlap is not a crane
count. The final baseline uses the cap of three per block.
The cap does not add spatial state. T arget-area attributes are predictors, not hard inventory
constraints. The simulator does not bind a container to a storage block. It also does not cre-
ate relocation work after reassignment. Closing T22 spreads its statistical demand across the
remaining blocks. It does not model feasibility, crane movement, or added truck travel. Sce-
nario A tests the resource-pool abstraction. It does not forecast a physical T22 closure.
Scenario Control Flow Generalises Only Within a Fixed Activity Set The Inductive-
Miner loop can generate sequential combinations and repetitions that were not present as com-
plete training variants. This is less restrictive than the earlier trie representation, but it is not
an open-ended terminal model. A scenario cannot create a new activity type, an unrecorded
exception mechanism, or a structurally different response to disruption. The two experiments
therefore test parameter changes inside the discovered activity language. Missing structural
mechanisms may still remove real sources of delay.

===== PDF PAGE 102 =====
Systematic Turnaround Under-Prediction The final model under-predicts mean turnaround
by 8.170 minutes and P90 by 17.310 minutes. The narrow seed intervals show that this is model
bias, not simulation noise. The log cannot show how much missing time comes from move-
ment, traffic, queueing, or an unrecorded state. Correct case order does not remove this limit
on KPI forecasts.
Scenario Intervals Cover Only Part of Structural Uncertainty The matched-
seed intervals in T able5.13cover random variation in one fixed model. They do not cover a
new training period, another discovery run, or missing spatial state. The cap sets a ceiling but
adds no location feasibility or equipment movement. More seeds would narrow these intervals.
They would not reduce structural uncertainty. The matched state-configuration ablation ad-
dresses one part of that uncertainty: it shows that the unresolved mean-turnaround response
persists with workload-blind rules. It still does not cover a new training period, another discov-
ered net, alternative resource semantics, or the missing spatial and queueing state.
External V alidity Is Limited to One Terminal and Time Window The temporal
hold-out is one later period from the same terminal and source systems. Near-perfect replay fit-
ness shows structural stability in this period, but the observed 2.450-minute mean-turnaround
shift shows that temporal stationarity does not hold even within the study window. The results
do not cover another terminal, season, gate regime, or infrastructure setup. That would require
another full discovery and validation run; an adaptive or rolling-window simulator would ad-
ditionally require prospective evaluation.

Conclusion
This thesis investigated the applicability, historical fidelity, and limitations of automatically dis-
covered data-aware process simulation for container-terminal truck operations. The work was
motivated by the increasing availability of operational event data and by recent advances that de-
rive executable simulation models directly from recorded process behaviour. These approaches
have primarily been studied in conventional business-process settings; their transfer to physical
logistics systems with spatial constraints, congestion, and resource dependencies remains less
established.
T o investigate this problem, the thesis developed a simulation-ready event log from two
months of continuous truck-handling data from the HHLA Container T erminal Burchardkai
and applied the ProSiT simulation-discovery framework. The resulting models were evaluated
on a temporally held-out period that was unseen during model development. The evaluation
combined control-flow conformance, distributional quality measures, repeated Monte-Carlo
simulation, and explicit structural checks. Different levels of contextual model expressiveness
were compared, and the final data-aware model was subsequently used in two transparent what-
if experiments concerning an RMG resource-pool intervention and increased truck demand.
The implementation and empirical evaluation answer the four research questions intro-
duced in Chapter 1.
RQ1 asked to what extent a ProSiT simulation model discovered from historical CTB truck-
operation data can reproduce the behaviour observed in an unseen temporal hold-out. The
model reproduces some dimensions considerably better than others. The final baseline achieves
94

===== PDF PAGE 104 =====
an unweighted yard-service W asserstein distance of 3.109 minutes and a real-frequency-weighted
distance of 2.366 minutes, while the inter-arrival distribution differs by only 0.072 minutes.
Despite these comparatively small distances, the model systematically under-predicts complete
truck turnaround. Mean turnaround is 31.57 minutes in the simulation compared with 39.74
minutes in the hold-out, an under-prediction of 8.17 minutes. The simulated 90th percentile
is 53.69 minutes compared with 71.00 minutes in the hold-out.
The descriptive transition analysis supplies an important scale check for this bias. Route-
specific fifth-percentile baselines range from 2.16 to 5.28 minutes, while the route-frequency-
weighted inbound and outbound components sum to 7.62 minutes per visit. This is close to
the 8.17-minute mean deficit and supports omitted physical transition time as a plausible ma-
jor contributor. Because these lower-tail baselines do not separate movement, traffic, resource
waiting and other operational delay, they cannot be used as a case-level correction or causal
decomposition.
The temporal-transfer audit adds a second explanation that must not be merged with move-
ment. Real mean turnaround rises by 2.450 minutes from training to the hold-out, and mean
service time rises for all nine yard activities. For the final cap-three baseline, the 8.170-minute
held-out mean deficit decomposes into a 5.721-minute discrepancy relative to the training-period
mean and the 2.450-minute observed period shift. The simulator is therefore already too fast
relative to its calibration period, while temporal drift makes the later comparison worse. The
two mechanisms are empirically distinguishable at the aggregate mean level but not identifiable
case by case.
RQ2 investigated the suitability of automatically discovered control flow for the CTB truck
process. When the explicit XES event order is preserved, the Inductive Miner discovers a com-
pact Petri net with no parallel operator. Its loop permits varying sequential combinations and
repetitions of yard activities, which is consistent with the domain requirement: the internal or-
der may vary, but one truck cannot perform two activities simultaneously. The model attains
a held-out fitness of 0.999992, fits 99.994 % of held-out traces completely, and retains a gen-
eralisation score of 0.9413. Its lower precision of 0.7558 reflects a broader sequential language,
not forbidden concurrency. The principal methodological finding is that event-order seman-
tics must be protected during log conversion. The earlier apparent parallelism was caused by
timestamp-based reordering, not by the authoritative XES discovery. After passing a hard struc-
tural eligibility check for parallel operators and the simulation-output contract checks, the un-
changed automatically discovered net is suitable as the ProSiT backbone and does not require
a manual prefix-trie replacement. Its permissive silent bypass is disclosed as a finding rather

===== PDF PAGE 105 =====
than removed through an additional physical constraint. As a separate robustness experiment,
a precision-oriented state split removes only that bypass. Held-out fitness remains 0.999992
while precision rises from 0.7558 to 0.7939; generalisation falls from 0.9413 to 0.8859. This
explicit trade-off shows that structural repair can improve the unsupported language without
pretending that the repaired net remains a purely automatic discovery.
RQ3 asked how increasing the contextual expressiveness of the simulation model affects
held-out fidelity. The three-level ablation separates process- and case-state rules from work-
load information. Relative tono-rules, strict workload-blindrulesreduce activity-rate L1 error
by 0.0103 in paired ten-seed comparisons and suppress the rarely sampled silent gate-only by-
pass. Their 0.115-minute turnaround-EMD improvement is not statistically resolved. Adding
workload information afterward yields no resolved weighted service-time improvement, in-
creases turnaround EMD by 0.303 minutes, and increases activity-rate L1 error by 0.0614. The
model’s short tail also becomes slightly shorter at each step. The best state abstraction is there-
fore process- and metric-specific: more data awareness is not automatically more valid state. In
CTB, the available workload proxies do not transfer as a useful representation of the omitted
physical queueing and movement mechanisms.
RQ4 asked how the discovered simulation model responds to transparent operational in-
terventions and what these responses reveal about its suitability for what-if decision support.
Both interventions can be implemented, inspected, and reproduced consistently. Removing
T22 from the simulated RMG resource pool eliminates all assignments to that resource, while
increasing the working-time arrival intensity by 20 % produces the intended change in the un-
derlying arrival parameter. Nevertheless, neither intervention produces a statistically resolved
change in mean truck turnaround time. The experiments do distinguish handling patterns: un-
der higher demand, mean RMG service rises by 0.091 minutes and mean receive service by 0.073
minutes, with both paired intervals excluding zero. For the workload-blind rules model, by con-
trast, RMG pre-service rises by +0.118 [0.038, 0.199] minutes and delivery pre-service by +0.146
[0.016, 0.276] minutes, while mean turnaround and the headline difference-in-differences re-
main unresolved. No T22-removal mean-effect interval excludes zero. Repeating both sce-
narios with the workload-blind rules bundle again produces no resolved mean-turnaround
response, and none of the headline difference-in-differences between state configurations ex-
cludes zero. The small component effects are therefore conditional on the current model and
do not establish physical causality. A separate capacity-pressure diagnostic further shows that
the 20 % intervention leaves the most-loaded held-out RMG block at only 0.506 nominal mean
utilisation under the three-task ceiling; the smallest multiplier to mean saturation is 2.37. The

null turnaround result therefore reflects both a modest average-capacity stress and a process
abstraction without the state needed to propagate short-period pressure. RMG resources rep-
resent statistical capacity assignments rather than physical container locations, and the model
does not represent container relocation, equipment movement, waterside and landside crane
allocation, or explicit spatial queue states. Consequently, the scenarios are valid experiments
on the discovered simulation model, but their results cannot be interpreted as experimentally
established counterfactual effects of the physical terminal.
Read jointly, the findings reveal a consistent boundary between patterns that are present in
the event log and mechanisms that are absent from the model. The stable sequential control-
flow structure and strong calendar pattern are patterns that the discovery pipeline can repro-
duce. By contrast, the weak duration signal for dominant RMG activities, the observed train–
test drift, the systematic turnaround deficit, and the lack of a congestion response under higher
demand all point to missing event-level and spatial state. This agreement across independent
diagnostics strengthens the interpretation, but it is not a causal proof: the analyses show which
explanation is consistent with the evidence and which stronger claims the evidence cannot sup-
port.
This boundary is not unique to the CTB implementation. Recent work on Process-Mining-
supported simulation in production and logistics reports that event logs with operation start
and end times can supply processing-time parameters, whereas transport, waiting, inventory,
machine state, and interacting material objects commonly require additional operational data
and semantic integration [16, 17]. The CTB results turn that general requirement into mea-
sured evidence: the transition-time scale check, component bottleneck ranking, capacity-pressure
audit, and state ablation show which missing mechanisms materially constrain the present
model’s interpretation.
In relation to recent data-aware Process Mining, the main advance demonstrated here is
therefore not an automatic increase in prediction accuracy. It is the ability to represent, inspect,
and intervene on conditional relationships while retaining an event-log-based process model.
The CTB case shows that this transparency is useful precisely when additional context fails: it
allows the researcher to distinguish a weak or unstable association from an omitted physical
mechanism. Data awareness expands what can be modelled, but the empirical content and in-
formation timing of the attributes determine whether that additional expressiveness improves
a held-out simulation.
Overall, the thesis shows that automatically discovered data-aware process simulation can be
transferred to container-terminal truck operations and can produce a transparent, reproducible,
97

===== PDF PAGE 107 =====
and structurally consistent simulation model from operational event data. At the same time,
the case study demonstrates that successful process discovery, historical fidelity, and scenario
executability are different requirements. High control-flow conformance does not guarantee
accurate temporal behaviour, additional contextual features do not necessarily improve simula-
tion fidelity, and an executable what-if intervention does not establish that the corresponding
physical effect is represented correctly.
The layered validity audit is the main academic outcome. Event semantics and sequential
control flow are supported; historical stochastic fidelity is mixed; temporal transfer is unstable;
physical resource–space semantics and scenario propagation are not yet validated. This rank-
ing avoids both extremes: the model is more than an unusable failed simulator because it pro-
vides strong structural and diagnostic evidence, but it is less than a decision-valid digital twin
because its counterfactual mechanisms are incomplete. The negative ablation and stress-test
results identify exactly which layer fails and where a model extension must be evaluated.
This contribution is timely because a recent systematic review identifies no shared validation
procedure for Process-Mining-based simulation, reports that 36 of 105 reviewed studies provide
no validation, and calls for assessments that connect process-model quality, stakeholder expec-
tations, historical outcomes, and established simulation-validation principles [15]. The thesis
does not claim to establish a universal framework from one terminal. It contributes a repro-
ducible, falsification-oriented implementation of such an assessment and demonstrates why re-
porting only replay fitness or one aggregate KPI would have produced an academically weaker
and operationally misleading conclusion.
The contribution of this thesis is therefore not a demonstration that the discovered model
is already sufficient for unrestricted operational decision support. Rather, it provides empiri-
cal evidence about where a recent white-box simulation-discovery approach transfers success-
fully to a complex logistics environment and where the abstraction reaches its limits. For the
CTB truck process, the results indicate that further progress towards trustworthy operational
decision support requires richer representations of spatial process state, physical resource con-
straints, equipment movement, and queueing mechanisms in addition to the event-log-based
behavioural information captured by the current simulation model.
This limitation does not diminish the operational rationale for the approach. A validated
terminal simulation should allow planners to translate forecast conditions into prospective de-
cisions: compare alternative hourly truck-slot limits or resource-priority policies, observe their
consequences for receive and delivery handling as well as total turnaround, and select a policy
before committing it to live operations. Automatically discovered data-aware models can make
98

===== PDF PAGE 108 =====
this loop easier to update and audit than a wholly manual model. The CTB case implements
the transparent intervention-and-measurement loop; its negative stress-test results identify the
additional physical state required before the loop can support prescriptive decisions.
6.1 Future Work
Future research should retain the reproducible discovery-and-validation framework while ex-
tending and testing the process representation in seven directions:
• Complete the structural-repair experiment:Simulate the exported gate-bypass repair
across the same ten seeds and rerun the scenario tests. A discriminator-based precision
repair could then be investigated as an explicit extension of Vinci and de Leoni’s addition-
oriented framework, with language restriction accepted only if held-out fitness and sce-
nario behaviour remain satisfactory.
• Separate physical transit from residual delay:Add timestamped route or position
observations and model Dpre-service= Ttransit+ Dresidual. Transit should be generated once
through visible auxiliary transitions or a simulator extension, while the existing delay
distribution is fitted to the residual to avoid double counting. Sparse internal routes
require a pooled or hierarchical fallback rather than unsupported pair estimates.
• Represent sticky spatial resources and explicit queues:Bind a case’s target area to
feasible blocks, distinguish individual availability and performance, and represent truck
and equipment movement. The discovered process model can then be coupled with a
discrete-event or agent-based physical layer for constraints that resource labels alone do
not encode.
• Evaluate an object-centric event and simulation design:Reconstruct truck visits,
trucks, containers, yard blocks, equipment, and appointments as related objects and
associate each handling event with all participating objects. This could preserve con-
tainer identity across operations, retain multi-block visits, and support location-specific
assignment [38, 39]. It would not create missing physical observations: positions, move-
ments, queues, and changing relations must still be logged and incorporated into exe-
cutable multi-object semantics. Recent object-centric discovery work also shows that
cross-object causality and model interpretability remain active research problems [40].
99

===== PDF PAGE 109 =====
• Adapt to temporal change:Evaluate rolling-window or recency-weighted discovery
prospectively and repeat the three-level state ablation in each period. This would show
whether workload state becomes useful after drift-aware refitting or remains an unsuit-
able abstraction.
• V alidate bottleneck mechanisms:Use the time-burden ranking to select candidate lo-
cations, then distinguish service, access, queue, and movement through additional times-
tamps and controlled interventions. Expert walkthroughs should verify the causal plau-
sibility of each proposed propagation chain.
• V alidate operational policies:Represent appointment-slot release, forecast utilisation,
and receive/delivery priority policies explicitly. Candidate hourly admission rates should
be evaluated against activity-specific and terminal-wide KPIs. In addition to modest pol-
icy changes, the experiment design should include capacity-pressure levels that approach
and cross calibrated block saturation. Results must then be compared with controlled
historical or prospective outcomes before deployment.
