Balancing fitness and precision in process 
model repair: framework formalization, 
assessment, and benefits for simulation
Francesco Vinci1* and Massimiliano de Leoni1 
Abstract 
Process models are essential tools for Process Science, guiding stakeholders on process 
automation. They are also employed to engage stakeholders into discussion to ana-
lyze how the process currently is, with the goal of discussing compliance and perfor-
mance issues. They are also used as backbone to create process simulation models, 
with the goal of analyzing the “as-is” processes and of proposing alternative process 
models that guarantee improved performances. In all these settings, desirable models 
need to only allow all the legitimate behavior (high precision), while disallowing forbid-
den behavior (high fitness). Often, models fail to achieve these properties and need 
to be repaired. This paper proposes a model-repair framework that assembles process-
simulation techniques, Machine-learning models, and Explainable AI, aiming to pin-
point the parts of the process models that enable illegitimate behavior and/or disallow 
valid one. These indications are then used to automatically repair a model and achieve 
better balances fitness and precision. Our framework has been implemented and eval-
uated on several models for five processes. The experimental results show that we 
outperform the state of the art in terms of balancing fitness and precision. The practi-
cal relevance of this technique is further showcased by illustrating the benefits of our 
repairing process models as backbone to create more accurate simulation models, 
capable to simulate more realistic runs. The simulation of more realistic process runs 
ensures that any process simulation-driven process improvements lead to advance-
ments that exist in the real process world and do not remain the simulated world.
Keywords: Process model repair, Business process simulation, Machine learning, 
Explainable AI
Introduction
A process model is a foundational tool in process science, offering a comprehensive rep -
resentation of the sequences of activity executions that the process allows. Process mod-
els are used to engage process stakeholders into discussions on how processes should 
be executed, or are alternatively used as input for Process-aware Information Systems 
to automate processes. A process model is also the backbone of business process simu -
lation models (cf.  van der Aalst (2015)), which are basically process models extended

Desirable models need to be precise and only allow legitimate behavior, while they 
should be able to represent the valid executions that have been observed (high fitness). 
As an example, an imprecise process model would lead to a business simulation model 
that enables runs that would not be observed in reality, while a low-fitness model would 
ultimately cause some legitimate to never be simulated: in both cases, process analysts 
would gain insight on the basis of unrealistic simulations.
When process models may fail to achieve satisfactory level of fitness and precision, 
they need to be repaired. However, fitness and precision are often contrasting: improv -
ing one may worsen the other.
This paper reports on a novel control-flow model-repair framework that tries to 
improve the harmonic means of fitness and precision, so as to provide a better balancing 
between these two dimensions. The framework can be summarized by the schema illus -
trated in Fig.  1. The key idea is to leverage on Business Process Simulation: the original 
process model, namely before any repair, is used to generate a set of simulated traces. 
These traces are then compared with those of a real event log to highlight the differ -
ences. This comparison is based on training a Machine Learning (ML) model that, given 
an execution trace, is capable to classify whether it is from the real or simulated event 
log. The rationale is that, if the process model is characterized by high fitness and pre -
cision, the ML model is not capable to determine whether a trace belongs to the real 
or simulated event log. In other words, the lower is the harmonic means of fitness and 
precision in a process model, the higher is the accuracy of the ML model. When the ML 
model accuracy is high, it is possible to pinpoint which trace characteristics are used 
for the determination whether the trace is from the real or simulated event log. In this 
paper, we use the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method to pinpoint the dif -
ferences. These trace characteristics are then employed to automatically improve the 
process model, aiming to achieve a process model that, once simulated, would produce 
traces that are indistinguishable from the real event log.

The framework includes a Repair  step (cf.  Fig.  1), where approaches need to be 
operationalized to use the recommendations for repair. In  Vinci and de Leoni (2024), 
we proposed two different approaches for repairing the model, hereafter referred to as 
basic and greedy. However, these approaches assume that the same activity cannot be 
repeated in different parts of the model. In this article, we propose a third, alternative 
approach that lifts this assumption, which is based on log-model alignments.
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed technique, we conducted evalu -
ations on five distinct real-life processes and various models, which were literature 
hand-made or mined models. Two of the literature hand-made models are character -
ized by same activities being present in different parts of the model, which could only 
be improved via the new approach that, leveraging on log-model alignments, allow the 
same activity to appear at different model’s parts
The results showcase the technique’s ability to enhance the original process models, 
improving on both fitness and precision. The evaluation also compares the resulting 
repaired models with respect to those obtained by  Fahland and van der Aalst (2015), 
again highlighting how we achieve a better balance between fitness and precision.
This article also reports on the benefits of better balancing fitness and precision when 
one aims at business process simulation: the repaired model is being used as basis to cre-
ate a simulation model. The evaluation of the same case studies illustrate that the result-
ing simulation model is more realistic: this is testified by the fact that the simulation 
model produces a set of process execution, in fact a simulated event log, which is closer 
to the original, real-life event log, compared with the simulated models built upon the 
original model and upon the model repaired by the technique by Fahland and van der 
Aalst (2015).

Preliminaries
This section introduces the preliminary concepts useful to discuss the framework. First, 
basic definitions of events, traces, and event logs are presented, subsequently, a subsec -
tion recalls the notions on Petri nets. A final subsection provides the definitions related 
to the explainability of a Machine Learning model.
Events, traces, event logs
Usually process transactional data are collected as event logs. An event log is a collec -
tion of traces, where a trace is a sequence of events with their attributes. In this paper, 
we abstract the events as the activities that are executed, thus ignoring the other event 
attributes because they are irrelevant for our technique.

In this paper we use Petri nets for modeling and representing processes with a set of 
recorded instances LA . We opted for Petri nets because they have a simple yet formal

Definition 4 (Marking). A marking m of a Petri net (P, T , F ) is a function m :P → N

soundness criteria introduced in van der Aalst (1998), according to which a Petri net

• Option to complete: For any reachable marking m, it is possible to reach marking mF.
• Proper completion: The only reachable marking that contains a token in the final 
place o is the marking mF.
• Absence of dead transitions: There are no dead transitions, i.e. it does not exist a tran-
sition that is not enabled at any reachable marking.
Log‑model alignments
Event logs are sequences of events, each associated to an activity label, while replaying 
a Petri nets generates firing sequences of transitions. Our repairing technique requires 
one to unambiguously map events with their activity labels to Petri-net transition firings. 
The Petri-net labelling function /afii9838 , which associates activity labels to transitions, allows 
for two transitions to be associated with the same activity labels. In this case, the map -
ping is far from being trivial, because the exact association of an event to a transition 
in case of multiple transitions with the same label require to look at the entire trance 
as a whole, knowing that some transitions may not have a label, and the corresponding 
events do not leave a trail in the event log.
In literature, this problem can be coped with by constructing an alignment of event log 
and process model: events in the event log need to be related to model elements and vice 
versa. An alignment relates moves in log and to moves in model as explained in the fol -
lowing definition. Here, we explicitly indicate no move with ≫.

Explanations of ML models
In Machine and Deep Learning, the highest accuracy is often achieved by using complex 
models that are difficult to interpret and, therefore, are often referred to as black box 
models. In a general sense, a classifier or regressor can be abstracted as a function ψ 
that returns the estimated value of the target variable, defined over a domain Y. The ψ ’s

Several approaches have been introduced to address the problem of the accuracy-
interpretability trade-off. One of the most widespread is based on computing Shapley 
values, which can be efficiently computed via the method SHAP (SHapley Additive

each potential element x = (x1, ... , xn) ∈ X1 × ... × Xn : given a feature fi , the Shapley

i  intuitively expresses how much the feature value xi contributes to the model

other feature is either discarded or retained. The base value is computed as the average 
of the predictions when a given multiset X ∈ B(X1 ×···× Xm) is used as input (usually 
the model’s training set).
Shapley values are computed for individual input vectors and individual features. 
However, we need to obtain a general contribution value for each individual feature, 
fairly independent of the specific input. We thus introduce the following explanation 
function, used thereafter in the paper, that returns the average of the Shapley values 
when a feature assumes a certain value.

Framework
The overall idea of this paper is to run simulations over a process model, to obtain a 
simulated event log and then compare it to the real one using a Machine Learning dis -
criminator model. Then, SHAP is used to compute the discriminating features, pinpoint 
the differences between the two event logs, and repair the control-flow process model in 
an automated iterative way.
The framework can be divided into four steps (see Fig.  1). In Step 1, the initial process 
model is used to generate a simulated event log. In Step 2, the traces of the real and sim -
ulated event logs are encoded yielding to a dataset used by a ML discriminator model to 
detect the inaccuracies of the process model. Step 3 focuses on the identification of the 
discriminating features, measuring the SHAP feature importance, which is then used in

The constructed dataset may have variables with a high correlation, leading to collin -
earity. When collinearity exists, it can be a challenge to interpret the individual effects 
of these variables on the target variable. In fact, it becomes difficult to discern whether a 
feature has a significant impact on the target variable independently or whether its effect 
is captured by other correlated features in the model. To overcome these issues, a feature 
selection is performed, considering the correlation between the features: the framework 
iterates across the features, and for each one, it considers the set of the features with an 
absolute correlation greater than a given threshold, only retaining one for each of these 
correlation groups, whose choice can be arbitrarily done 3. Furthermore, the features 
that are associated with a variance lower than a fixed threshold are removed. The valida-
tion set is then constructed following the same procedure and using the same features 
selected by the feature selection phase described above.

the model has to be intended as the probability that the input trace is real. The model is

Step 3: identification of the discriminating features
Once the model is trained, we create a multiset X true that is obtained from the training-
set instances that the ML model has correctly classified, projected over the independent 
features (i.e., removing the target variable) 4. This is done in order to understand the set 
of features that enable a correct discrimination. We compute the impact of the features 
in the output for the samples in X true to provide explanations of certain classifications

The remainder of this section proposes three different approaches to repair the pro -

feature fa>b ∈ R . “The basic approach” section reports on a first basic approach, which 
requires each visible transition to be associated to a different label, namely two different 
visible transitions can be associated to the same label. The basic approach repairs the 
Petri net by introducing several new transitions, which can be slow to perform, on the 
one hand, and can potentially hamper the net’s precision and simplicity, on the other 
hand. Greedy approach introduced in “Greedy repair” section implements some heuris-

approach, although this might negatively affect the quality of the repairing model. Last 
and very importantly, “Repair via log-model alignments” section proposes an alternative 
approach based on log-model alignments, which allows multiple visible transitions to be 
associated to the same activity labels.

To tackle this challenge, we propose a greedy approach to include relation a >N b 
between two transitions ta, tb ∈ T . This greedy approach comes from the observation 
that, given any marking ma ∈ GN reached after firing ta and any marking mb ∈ GN where 
tb is enabled, it is sufficient to add arc (ma, τ , mb) to formally incorporate a >N b into 
the Petri net. In particular, the approach picks the marking ma with the shortest path 
in GN from the initial marking mI , along with marking mb with the short path to the 
final marking mF . The approach is motivated by the fact that, by selecting such these two 
nodes, fewer paths are included in the reachability graph: this leads to a minimization of 
the behaviour additionally included into the Petri net model, thereby reducing the nega -
tive impacts on the precision.

Experimental setup
The framework has been implemented in Python: it takes in input an event log in XES 
format, and a Petri net in Petri Net Markup Language (PNML)11.
The event log is temporally split into train, validation, and test sets: with respectively 
60% , 20% and 20% of the total traces. The training and the validation logs are used by 
the framework as described in “Framework ” section, while the test log is employed for 
evaluation. We also implemented a Stochastic Petri net simulator in Python for the 
simulation phase (cf. “Step 1: process simulation ” section)12. In Step 2, CatBoost (Prok -
horenkova et al. 2018), a ML method based on gradient boosting over decision trees, is 
used as discriminator model. SHAP values are computed using the Python’s implemen -
tation13. The validation set/log were also used to perform hyper-parameter optimization 
during each framework iteration to avoid overfitting and/or underfitting.
During the experiments, we monitored the accuracies in the training and validation 
sets of the discriminator model during each framework iteration in order to avoid over -
fitting and/or underfitting. In general, we found optimal results using depth equal to 3, a 
learning rate of 0.01, and training for 150 iterations.

Related works
The work by Fahland and van der Aalst (2015) is among the first ones that address the 
model-repair problem to enhance fitness, but is not the only one. Polyvyanyy et al. (2016) 
puts forward methods to define process model repair as an optimization problem, where 
costs are assigned to various repair actions. However, this work aims to maximizing the 
fitness, as authors also clearly state, relegating precision as second-class citizen.
Mitsyuk et al. (2017) propose a model-repair approach able to achieve a high level of 
fitness, but, as authors themselves admit, at a cost of dropping precision. Other works 
address the model repair and try to improve the model precision (Kalenkova et al. 2020; 
Rennert and van der Aalst 2024).
In a recent work, Genga et  al. (2024) address the challenge of balancing fitness and 
precision by proposing a repair methodology based on anomalous local instance graphs. 
However, their approach assumes the presence of a user that chooses among a set of 
anomalous instances the one to be used for repairing. We then decided to compare our 
framework with the work of Fahland and van der Aalst (2015) since it is the one, with an 
available implementation, proposing the problem of repairing process models similarly 
as our approach.
Other existing literature proposes interactive frameworks for process model repair, in 
which possible differences between the model and the log are displayed to the user who 
manually repairs the process model (Armas Cervantes et al. 2017; Pourbafrani and van 
der Aalst 2022). These research works do not aim at an automated model repair, which 
serves a different purpose: repairing the model, using feedback from humans.
Repair a process model is also linked to concept drift (cf. Revoredo (2023)): one wants 
to repair the model to align it with the behaviour after, i.e. when the process changes 
over time and then the model does not align the reality anymore.  Revoredo (2023) 
framed process model repair as a theory revision problem. In this work, the event log 
and the process model are partitioned to repair only changeable parts of the model that 
are manually selected by practitioners, considering only traces representing acceptable 
behaviour. In case of concept drift one can select the part of the model subject of the 
process changes, and repair that.
Incremental process discovery (see, e.g., Schuster et al. (2023)) is also related to model 
repair, but the goal is different as the former does not aim to obtain models that are as 
close as possible to a reference original process model.
Meneghello et al. (2022) proposed a framework that uses decision-tree models to dis -
criminate the traces observed in the event log from those allowed by a simulation model. 
The resulting decision trees should be used as input to subsequently change the sim -
ulation model in multiple iterations, so that ultimately the traces of the event log and 
those obtained from simulation cannot be distinguished, namely the resulting decision 
tree has very low accuracy. However, the changes need to be manually implemented 

===== PAGE 29 / 33 =====

Vinci and de Leoni  Process Science             (2025) 2:3 
 
by process analysts, a step that is sometimes difficult, because the decision tree gives 
ambiguous explanations.
Note that repairing a model can be regarded as ensuring soundness of the model 
(see, e.g., Gambini et al. (2011); Lohmann and Fahland (2014)), which is a relevant, yet 
orthogonal problem.
Towards multi‑perspective process model repair
An avenue of future work is to not limit the repair to the control-flow perspective, but to 
extend it to consider the other perspectives, as well. This can be achieved by including 
features in the discriminator that refer to other perspectives: 
 Resource Perspective. For each transition T, a resource categorical feature can be 
incorporated, which takes on a value equal to the name of the resource that per -
formed A. If transition T is performed multiple times, one needs to approximate 
it as, e.g., the last resource performing A. Adding a feature for each pair of transi -
tion and resource would likely generate too many features, with a high risk of lead -
ing to overfitting. If a discrimination is found on the feature for, e.g., T, the set of 
resources allowed to perform T is changed. Namely, if resource R performs T, then 
the trace is real or, resp., from the simulated log, then R is added or, resp., removed 
from the set of resources allowed to perform T.
 Time Perspective. For each transition T, a numerical feature can be added to 
indicate the execution duration of instances of T (or, average if executed multi -
ple times). If a discrimination is found on the feature for a specific transition, the 
modelling of the distribution of T’s duration is considered incorrect, which thus 
needs to be (re-)discovered from data, as, e.g., indicated in Camargo et al. (2020). 
Currently, directly-follow features in Definition 8 are boolean-typed, to which true 
and false values are assigned depending on whether or not the sequence of two 
transitions is observed in a trace (cf. Eq.  2), respectively. The boolean value can 
potentially be replaced by the time elapsed between two subsequent transitions, 
thus being able to discriminate on the basis of the transition’s waiting times. How-
ever, waiting time might be related to exogenous conditions and events that are 
outside the “universe” of the process model, or alternatively to the unavailability 
of resources. The latter case is clearly related to the resource perspective, more 
than to the time. It is thus challenging to (semi)automatically repair the model to 
accommodate the correct waiting times. In a spirit of separation of concerns, one 
could firstly fix the resource perspective: this would mean that the resulting wait -
ing time is related to exogenous conditions, annotating the arc with waiting-time 
characterization (simple averages, distributions, etc.).
 Data Perspective. The relevant aspects related to the data perspective of a pro -
cess model are largely focused on the data-driven decisions that determine how 
process instances are executed. These decisions are typically modeled via guards 
attached to transitions (cf. de Leoni (2022)). For each transition T with a guard G, 
a feature can be included that indicate whether G evaluated true when T was per -
formed. Similarly to the time perspective, if a discrimination is found of a guard-

===== PAGE 30 / 33 =====

Page 30 of 33Vinci and de Leoni  Process Science             (2025) 2:3 
related feature, that is an indication that the guard was likely wrong and would be 
required to be rediscovered.
Conclusion
A process model is one of the key ingredients for several techniques for the analysis, 
improvement and enactment of processes. A process model is also employed to engage 
with process’ stakeholders into discussion on how the process is being executed, possibly 
in comparison with how it ought be carried out.
For any of the purposes that we just mentioned, the process model needs to reason -
ably summarize all the legitimate behavior (high fitness), and only that (high precision). 
Unfortunately, process models, either hand-made or mined from event logs, are often -
times characterized by not scoring well in both fitness and/or precision. In fact, obtain -
ing a model with high fitness is sometimes contrasting with having one with precision.
This article introduced a model-repair framework that aims to improve an initial pro -
cess model so as to maximize the harmonics means of fitness and precision. In fact, a 
higher harmonic means guarantees that their balance is better. This is a novelty with 
respect to the largest body of research on model repair (cf.  “Related works” section), 
which conversely tends to give more importance to fitness (typically), or to precision 
(seldom).
In summary, our framework idea is as follows. The framework uses two input ele -
ments: a process model, represented as a Petri net, and an event log. The process model 
is initially used to generate a simulated event log, which is then compared with a real 
event log to pinpoint differences. The differences are unveiled by training a ML dis -
criminator model. Since an accurate process model would generate logs that are indis -
tinguishable from the original event log, the ML model should perform poorly. If it is 
conversely able to discriminate, explainable AI techniques, specifically SHAP , are used to 
explain and pinpoint the discriminatory model features. These features are now action -
able to allow for an automated model repair: we illustrate how to use them to this aim. 
Three approaches are being proposed for the automated model repair, two of which were 
previously introduced in Vinci and de Leoni (2024): one basic and complete, one more 
greedy in deciding how to repair. However, both the approaches are actionable when 
the process model does not contain multiple visible transitions of the same label. This 
assumption holds when models are mined via the major process discovery algorithms, 
such as Inductive Miner (Leemans et al. 2013) or Split Miner (Augusto et al. 2017). How-
ever, several hand-made models do not match this assumptions, such as two out of the 
five used in evaluation.
The evaluation was based on five processes and several models, hand-made taken from 
the literature or mined through Inductive Miner. The evaluation shows that our repair 
technique can produce better models - in terms of harmonic means of fitness and preci -
sion - than what alternative, state-of-the-art techniques would produce, which are also 
better than the original models.
This article is an extension of our previous work by  Vinci and de Leoni (2024), and 
provides the following additional contributions:

1. An alternative approach is proposed, which is based on log-model alignments and 
can also repair process models with multiple visible transition of the same label (cf. 
“Repair via log-model alignments” section).
2. We performed experiments on four new models, for two case studies, from  Man -
nhardt (2018) and their derivatives (cf. “Case studies” section), which our framework 
is only able to repair thanks to the introduction of the new repair approached based 
on log-model alignments.
3. Not only does the evaluation show successful application of our technique to pro -
duce better repaired models, but also does it show the benefits in real settings when 
the goal is to use process models as starting point to create a simulation: the simula -
tion models created on the basis of our repair process models are more realistic than 
those obtained from the original process models (cf. “Experimental results: simula -
tions quality” section).
4. On the presentation side, the article is enriched (i) through numerous examples to 
better describe the framework, and the proposed evaluation approaches, and also (ii)  
includes some figures of original and repaired process models, which allow readers 
to appreciate how our framework can produce simpler models.
5. Analyses and experiments were conducted to successfully assess the robustness of 
the repair technique to noise and the scalability to models of larger size. The theoreti-
cal and test-based analyses of complexity are indeed reported in “Greedy repair” and 
“Experimental results: noise robustness and scalability” sections, respectively.
A definitive assessment of the accuracy of simulation models (cf. item 3 in the list above) 
has to consider the simulation stochasticity: one single simulation might not be repre -
sentative. To tackle this aspect, we performed several simulation runs and conducted 
t-tests for assessing the representativeness of the results obtained (cf. “Experimental 
results: simulations quality” section).
This work assumes that the simulated event logs are nearly complete with respect to 
the process model  (Yang et  al. 2012). We acknowledge that this assumption can be a 
threat of validity, and we aim in the future to evaluate the completeness of the simulated 
event log. Currently, we only repair models by allowing addition behavior, while it might 
potentially be the case in which a better balancing between fitness and precision can be 
achieved by restricting behavior: so do we aim to extend the technique so as to restrict 
behavior.
